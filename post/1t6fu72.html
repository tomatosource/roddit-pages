Sac fly “home runs”

Yesterday, the Reds took a 6-4 lead in the top of the 9th on a 2-run sac fly. I noted in the comments that runners scoring from 2nd on a sac fly is a surprisingly common occurrence, but what I failed to note is that most of the time it happens because of an error. See, Rule 9.08(d) stipulates that “[the Official Scorer shall] score a sacrifice fly when, before two are out, the batter hits a ball in flight handled by an outfielder or an infielder running in the outfield in fair or foul territory that

(1) is caught, and a runner scores after the catch, or

(2) is dropped, and a runner scores, if in the scorer’s judgment the runner could have scored after the catch had the fly been caught.”

Basically, a sac fly is awarded on either what you normally think a sac fly is, or what would be a sac fly if the fielder hadn’t muffed the catch. The former is the rarer scenario when a sac fly scores a runner from 2nd, but it still does happen relatively often. I’ll have to do a writeup about it at some point.

In any case, the second half of the rule means that a sac fly can be awarded even when the batter gets on base. And by extension, a batter can hypothetically make it to any base while still being awarded a sac fly. With that in mind, I’d like to talk about the three times since 1910 a batter hit a sac fly and still made a full trip around the base paths. In effect, a sac fly “home run”.

  1. June 9, 1915, New York Giants @ St. Louis Cardinals

It’s the top of the 5th and the Giants are ahead 3-2. There are men on second and third, courtesy of a single, walk, and botched pickoff attempt thrown cleanly by the catcher to second base but completely missed by the shortstop. At bat is Larry Doyle.

Previously, in the top of the third, Doyle had hit a ball to the center fielder Cozy Dolan, whose failed attempt at a shoestring catch led to the ball rolling to the wall for an RBI triple to put the Giants up 2-0. Moreover, Doyle’s advance home on a George Burns single represented the Giants’ last run scored up to this point. Now, Doyle was going to humiliate Dolan again.

On a routine fly ball, to quote the New York Times, “[Dolan] set himself under the same Doyle’s long fly, evidently intent on the throw he was going to make to the plate to get Snodgrass. The ball dropped pleasantly into the outstretched paws of Dolan, bobbed as gracefully out of them, and rolled to the fence. Before the ball was retrieved all three men had counted.” It was now 6-2 Giants, and by the time the inning was over, it would be 9-2 in their favor.

The story doesn’t end there though; the Cardinals responded with 2 runs in the bottom of the 5th, and a 7-run bottom of the 7th put them over 11-9. The Giants would get a single run in during the top of the 8th, but that would be all they could muster as they lost 11-10 and squandered their 7-run lead.

As for Doyle, he had a spectacular game despite the loss. After his triple and sac fly, he hit a double in the top of the 6th, and that one Giants run in the top of the 8th was none other than Doyle’s home run. In other words, if Dolan had never made it to the ball in the first place, Doyle would likely have had a cycle. But at the end of the day, the sac fly home run is a rarer feat, so I can’t really be disappointed.

  1. May 17, 1986, Cleveland Indians @ Toronto Blue Jays

I wish I had video of this play but the week’s edition of This Week in Baseball only glosses over the series as a whole. Anyways, it’s the top of the 7th and the Indians are drastically behind 11-1, but a Tony Bernazard single, Andy Allanson single, and Eddie Williams groundball forceout has men on the corners with one out. At the plate is Julio Franco, in year 5 of a career that would span another 21.

Franco hits a fly ball to right on what should be a routine sac fly. However, as the Dover Times Reporter writes, “Barfield [RF] dropped the ball, allowing one run to score, and Moseby [CF] was unable to pick up the ball in repeated attempts as Eddie Williams scored from first and Franco circled the bases.” A comedy of errors by the two outfielders has allowed a routine sac fly to become an unearned inside the park “home run”, plating three. Ultimately though, the Jays’ lead would prove insurmountable as they handily won 11-5.

  1. September 25, 2011, Seattle Mariners @ Texas Rangers

It’s the bottom of the 5th and the Rangers are ahead 4-3. The first batter, Ian Kinsler, has just drawn a walk, stolen second on the first pitch of Esteban Germán’s at bat, then immediately stolen third on the pitch right after. After taking three consecutive balls inside to work his way to a full count, Germán hits a routine fly ball to center field.

Trayvon Robinson runs in to make the catch but the ball brushes past his glove as it bounds towards the wall. By the time Ichiro runs over from right field and gets to the ball, Germán is already well on his way to third. It’s clear that there will be no chance of gunning him down at home, and so Germán makes it without even an attempted throw to the catcher. It’s 6-3 Rangers, and by the time the game is done, the Rangers will have won 12-5. This time, you can find video of the play here: https://youtu.be/-wpYIoAlWRw?si=ETlouylbl5ByN_n7&t=5950

Now, there may be more sac fly “home runs” out there somewhere. However, since sac flies were first instituted as an official statistical category in 1908, and since a four-base sac fly is such a notable event that it is extraordinarily unlikely to be missing from Retrosheet’s event files, I think the only potential way there could be more is if they were in the 1908 and 1909 seasons. Given the fact that there have only been three of these in the 116 full seasons since, I think it’s more than likely that these are the only ones ever.
