A Retired Jersey for Every Team - Part 2: The Atlanta Hawks

Part 1 - L.A. Clippers

Welcome back to Part 2 of this ongoing offseason series! From here on out, we’ll be alternating in alphabetical to reverse alphabetical order for all the teams that have a retired jersey or two…or in the case of the Celtics, like, half of all numbers from zero to ninety-nine.

Starting off with the Atlanta Hawks, we’ve got a fairly reasonable pool of players to choose from. Trae Young is the most recent one to come to mind. 4x All-Star, 1x All-NBA 3rd Team point guard who led the league in assists and got the Hawks to their first Conference Finals in almost a decade. Sure, that was the high point for the Young era but you couldn’t have known at the time that the relatively young core (Pun not intended) would largely stagnate or that their front office would commit malpractice by trading for Dejounte Murray or salary dumping John Collins for essentially nothing - or more recently, not drafting Alex Sarr 1st overall, and none of these moves reflect poorly on Trae.

That said, it’s hard to overlook that the Trae-led Hawks highest high was an outlier when every other playoff appearance came out of the play-in - if they even got out of the play-in tournament, that is. Trae probably does deserve to get his jersey retired down the line but it’s a bit too early for that honor to go to him even if he did represent an entire era for the franchise.

But like I said, there’s a lot of candidates, some of whom that are either retired or getting close to it. There’s Al Horford, another 4x All-Star though one who was a part of the Hawks franchise-high, 60 win season and also has a Conference Finals appearance to his name. Unfortunately, his exit from the Hawks was decidedly less amiable and there’s been little done to mend the bridge between franchise and player.

In the end, it came down between Joe Johnson - the 1x All-NBA, 6x All-Star and face of the 2000s Hawks who just could never get to the Conference Finals like Trae or Al - and the 90s defensive stalwart at point in Mookie Blaylock…

…or at least it would if there wasn’t another candidate: The guy who coached Mookie int he 90s.

He was a Hawks in his playing days too.

Lenny Wilkens

TL;DR (Too Long, Dump the Resume):

  • 5x All-Star (1963-65, 1967-68)
  • 2nd in MVP voting in 1967⁄68
  • 1x Coach of the Year (1994)
  • Achieved the title of “Most Winningest Coach” at the time as head coach of the Atlanta Hawks

This ultimately came down to Mookie or the guy who coached him at the time and considering Lenny has a pretty solid playing career on top of being one of the best coaches in franchise history when factoring in the context of him helming a post-Dominique Wilkins Hawks team, I gave the nod to him despite him technically playing in St. Louis and not Atlanta, not to mention him playing in a much less competitive era.

Not to say that Lenny Wilkins isn’t deserving of praise - he’s in this series for a reason. One of the most accomplished players of all time at an inch above 6 feet, Lenny’s amongst the sharpest playmakers to ever hit the hardwood. A tenacious defender, unselfish he was one of the best guards of his time and made an immediate impact on the then-St. Louis Hawks in his rookie season; the Hawks improved from a win percentage of 61.8% (46 wins, 29 losses) to 64.6% (51 wins, 28 losses) and went back to the Finals, their fourth appearance in franchise history. They would not win against the Bill Russell Celtics but still, back-to-back Finals isn’t anything to sneeze at even with the relevant context of how few teams there were in the NBA at the time.

But if you want a more direct showcasing of the plucky point’s impact on winning, look no further than his sophomore season.

All 20 games of it.

The 1961-62 St. Louis Hawks were a bit a of a mess due to injuries to several players but the most important ingredient to that mess of a stew was….well you know how players of this era are often called plumbers and firefighters since they often had to take on second jobs to make ends meet? Lenny Wilkins was a reservist for the army and the Berlin Crisis of 1961 saw him get called up to serve, so he missed a lot of his second year with the Hawks as a second lieutenant.

After the Soviet Union constructed the Berlin Wall in August of 1961, the United States activated several Army National Guard and Reserve units. Among those called up was a young man named Lenny Wilkens. A former star college basketball player at Providence College, he was now playing for the St. Louis Hawks in the National Basketball Association.

Source: https://thewritelaunch.com/2020/09/peach-baskets/

Fun fact: This is the exact same event that saw fellow Hall of Famer Elgin Baylor serve in the U.S. Army. Anyway the short version is that St. Louis would miss the playoffs that season. Anyway, the good news is that, upon his return, Lenny blossomed into a perennial All-Star and the Hawks would once again be a force in the Western Division. They never did make it back to the Finals but they were making deep runs in the postseason - or at least deep for the 60s since there was only three rounds as opposed to the four we’re all used to - and Wilkens was a huge part of that in both the regular and postseason.

His best regular season was in 1967⁄68 where he averaged 20.3 points, 5.3 rebounds and 8.3 assists. They didn’t track turnovers back then so the numbers don’t show precisely how efficient he was as a playmaker but it was more than enough to earn him 2nd place in voting, behind Wilt Chamberlain in one of the Stilt’s better seasons. For reference, Wilt averaged 24.3⁄23.8⁄8.6 the same season. So no shame in that silver medal. If nothing else, it was a good note for his time to go out on as the Hawks would trade Lenny to the SuperSonics for Walt Hazzard. Not a great trade for the now-Atlanta-based Hawks as Walt would never catapult the Hawks to the same level of success though Lenny himself would never see the postseason again despite having many great regular seasons. But that’s a story for another time. All i’ll say is that the Sonics have retired Wilkens Number 19 despite having far less success as a player and as a coach than he did as a Hawk.

Oh yeah, his coaching days. Wilkins has a pretty lengthy coaching career that predates his time coaching with the Hawks (Then moved to Atlanta at the time of hiring) and broke ground as one of the first black head coaches in the NBA but to fast forward to his return to the Hawks, then-head coach Bob Weiss was fired shortly after Atlanta were swept aside by the 1993 Bulls and Lenny was hired as his replacement.

As an aside, I have to say that while it’s not too uncommon for players to become coaches, it’s decidedly more rare for a player of a given team to end up being their coach. Rarer still is for a former franchise legend to thrive and while Lenny had been a successful coach up to that point, success was by no means guaranteed as the 90s Hawks would have a major wrench thrown into its proceedings. Imagine if the Clippers hired Blake Griffin to coach their team and they were regularly winning 50 games after trading away Kawhi.

His then-3rd coaching stint in the NBA had…a bit of a rocky start. The Hawks went 1-4 in their first five games but they then rattled off a 14-game winning streak, capped off by a double-digit win against the Cleveland Cavalivers. By All-Star break, the Hawks were 34-13, holding the 1st seed in the Eas-wait what?

They did what?

What do you mean they traded Dominique Wilkins?!? Okay, so the Hawks franchise GOAT and one of the earliest superstar players to ever come back from a torn Achilles - an injury that was considered a career-ender at the time - was traded out of nowhere to the Clippers with a 1st for Danny Manning. For some context as to why this was so shocking - not just because the return…sucked - not only did the Hawks do this when they were 1 seed in the East but this was the season where Michael Jordan was retired, so there was a legitimate chance for Atlanta to be the team to come out of the East, especially since the Human Highlight Film™ was averaging a cool 26/6/2 with 1.2 steals. Yes, Nique was 34 years old but that level of production was what contributed to them having the record they did. It is still one of the most shocking trades for a star player and a lot of teams would have faltered after moving their best player for half a season of Danny Manning.

Not so for the 94 Hawks. They went 23-12 to end the season, a 65.7 win percentage which while a dip from their pre-All-Star record is nevertheless impressive considering they just traded their best player away. The aforementioned point guard Mookie Blaylock played brilliantly enough to earn one of his 6 All-Defense nods and the team managed to retain the 1 seed. Wilkins won Coach of the Year in decisive fashion and while the Hawks lost in 6 games to the upstart Pacers, it was a successful season through and through to have gotten as far as they did without one of the best players in the NBA.

If that sounds familiar, it’s because it’s sort of what happened to the Hawks this year, albeit they got better without Trae Young but that’s a story for another time. Lost in 6 games too, funny enough. anyway, the 1994⁄95 and 95⁄96 season were…a bit of a disappointing dip for the Hawks. They lost Manning in free agency in 94 and struggled to find a new identity beyond “Mookie is one of the best guard defenders of the era”. That said, they still went above 0.500 both seasons - even getting revenge against that same Pacers squad in the 1996 playoffs in an upset victory as the lower seed - and it wouldn’t be long before they would rebound by signing Nuggets center Dikembe Mutumbo in the 1996 offseason. Plus Wilkins wrenched away the title of “Most Winningest Coach” away from Red Auerbach on January 6th, 1995 with a dominant, double-digit beatdown of the Washington Bullets 112-90.

That, coupled with a late season trade to acquire their future All-Star in Christian Laettner led to a resurgence for Atlanta and with it, more 50+ win seasons in a new era. We don’t have time to go over every season in depth but just know that Wilkins managed to crank 50 or more wins out of several squads revolving around Mutumbo and Blaylock and made regular appearances into the 2nd round of the postseason throughout the 90s. His final season was less praise-worthy but by that point, Atlanta had decided to enter into a rebuild and did so with 3 firsts in the 1999 NBA Draft (Technically 4 but they traded away one of them to the 76ers) as well as trading away their leading scorer Steve Smith for a bunch of players who didn’t work out for them. They won 28 wins and Wilkins resigned from his position, later becoming the Toronto Raptors 4th ever head coach and leading them to their first ever playoff series win. Again, story for another time.

As a player, Lenny was a franchise cornerstone. As a coach, he kept the ship steady for the 90s Hawks as they transitioned into a new era. He held a win percentage of 57.2%, making him one of the best in franchise history. When you add in that he held that high a win percentage across nearly 550 games (310 wins, 212 losses in the regular seasons), then the conundrum of which of his numbers you put in the rafters (Lenny wore 3 different numbers with the Hawks) becomes irrelevant. Pick one out of a hat, it doesn’t matter because at least one of them deserves to be retired. Probably number 14 given it was the same number he wore in college and was retired by Providence College.

Lenny Wilkins passed away recently so his jersey would be retired posthumously but he’s deserving of that honor all the same. Legends never die, and Lenny most certainly was one, be it on the court as a player or behind the line getting the most out of Mookie and company.

Rest in peace.
