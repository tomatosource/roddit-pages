Some non-obvious statistical observations from the past NBA regular season

Alex Caruso - DPOY off the bench

Alex is still one of the most underrated players in the league; however, calling him underrated is still not bad form, unlike similar cases with Derrick White or, say, Jrue Holliday two years ago. The Bald Mamba’s prowess is not obvious and is hardly reflected in the usual “points-rebounds-assists” numbers. To appreciate his impact on Oklahoma City, we must pay attention to advanced statistics.

Over a sample of 41+ games played, 7+ DFGA (defensive field goal attempts) per game Alex has:

-DFG% - 45.3% (32nd among guards/swingmen);

-17th among guards/swingmen in defensive matchup difficulty with an average opponent’s FG% of 47.3%;

-DIFF% (the difference between a player’s DFG% and the average FG% of his matchups) - -2% (23rd place in the same category).

If we set the bar for a matchup’s FG% at 47%+, then Caruso would rank among players at his position:

-fourth in DFG% (behind Keldon Johnson, Aaron Naismith, and Stephon Castle);

-second among pure guards (not swingmen) in DIFF%, behind the aforementioned Stephon Castle.

Furthermore, according to various hustle and stock metrics among all players in the league (based on a sample of 41+ games), Alex:

-ranks twentieth in deflections - 3 per game on average (with only eighteen minutes on the court on average) and second in the same metric per 48 minutes;

-nineteenth place (eighth per 48 minutes) in loose ball recovered def – he saves the ball exactly two out of three times;

And now, pay attention – the most important thing!

Alex has the league’s highest defensive rating among players who have played more than half of the season’s games, and also the only one with a point allowed percentage below 100 points per 100 possessions – 99.3. Furthermore, his closest pursuer in adequate minutes, his Oklahoma City teammate Chet Holmgren, is a full three points behind Alex – he has 102.3 points allowed per 100 possessions. Pat Connathan is also in between, but he played only 7 garbage minutes per game for the Hornets, and his defensive rating is closer to Holmgren than to Caruso.

For comparison:

-Victor Wembanyama, DPOY 2026 - 103.6 DRTG;

-Rudy Gobert and Derrick White - 108 DRTG;

-Lou Dort - 108.2 DRTG;

-Amen Thompson - 110.4 DRTG.

And so on.

Here, of course, credit should be given to the Thunder’s systematic and well-developed defense, since even an individual player’s defensive rating is ultimately the result of teamwork and, in part, an indicator of the quality of his teammates. But here, as they say, cause and effect shouldn’t be confused. Caruso is the cornerstone of the defending champions’ second unit defense.

Caruso has the best net rating on the team - +17.6. Without him, it’s only +8.3. The defense drops significantly - without Caruso on the court, they allow 8.7 more points per 100 possessions than with him. Meanwhile, Oklahoma City’s average defense is 106.5.

And finally, here’s how Alex stopped the opponents’ key offensive options in matchups:

-Alperen Şengün - 6 of 10;

-Kawhi Leonard - 2 of 6;

-Kevin Durant - 2 of 6;

-Devin Booker - 3 of 7;

-De’Aaron Fox - 4 of 9;

-Victor Wembanyama - 2 of 5;

-Jaylen Brown - 3 of 9;

-Anthony Edwards - 3 of 9;

-Paolo Banchero - 3 of 6.

A premium matchup defender.

Good ball movement isn’t a guarantee of success. And even the opposite

It turns out that the old rule drummed into the heads of young basketball players from the very first days of training is actually more of a relic of the past or even wishful thinking. We’d like to think that teams that play fresh, inspired, and exciting games are more effective than those that lock down the offense with one or two players, mechanize all game processes, and automate the ability to smother opponents with their ABC play. But reality is much harsher than our romantic notions.

Few would argue that Oklahoma City is the best team in the league right now. But do you know where they rank in average assists per game? Nineteenth…

Okay, maybe it’s all due to the relatively slow pace of play (16th pace in the league). But what do percentages and advanced metrics say?

- 25th in AST℅ - only 59% of their field goals came from teammates’ assists;

- 18th in AST RATIO;

- 29th in 2FG assisted - 53.7%, meaning more than half of their two-pointers came from iso possessions;

- Second in average isolation plays per game - 10.9; third in their percentage of total attacks - nearly every tenth Thunder attack is a pure iso.

At the same time, they’re incredibly effective in these iso possessions. They’re first in points per possession - 1.03 PPP (not to mention the fact that Shai, by some coincidence, is the league’s most effective iso player). The Lakers have the same number, and the Nuggets, Cavs, and Timberwolves, who aren’t suffering from a lack of wins, have slightly fewer.

By the way, Boston, the second-place team in the East, has the fewest assists this season.

Okay, so that’s it.

But who, on the other hand, moves the ball the best?

There are as many as seven teams in the top 10 by AST% that missed the playoffs. These include the Warriors (1st place with a 70.6 AST%), the Jazz (2nd place with a 69.6%), the Pacers (5th place with a 67.8%), as well as the Grizzlies, Nets, Bulls, and Heat.

The Warriors, Pacers, Bulls, Grizzlies, and Heat are also in the top six in total assists per game.

The situation with potential assists changes slightly, but not dramatically. The top 10, along with the Hawks, Raptors, Cavs, Spurs, and Nuggets, includes the Warriors, Jazz, Bulls, Heat, and Grizzlies. Meanwhile, Houston is 20th, Phoenix is ​​21st, Magic are 22nd, Celts are 24th, Lakers are 28th, and Wolves are 29th.

The top teams are increasingly focusing on defense, concentrating their offensive strength in two or three key areas. Isolations, drive-and-kicks, pick-and-rolls with ball-handler finishes are the main offensive trends in modern basketball, as is sweaty butting of butts for rebounds on the opposing backboard.

And we’ll have to come to terms with it.

Long-Armed Versatile Players Are the New Faces of the League, or How Andrei Kirilenko Foreshadowed Modern Basketball

Just look at the current Atlanta Hawks, who tore the league apart in the second half of the season after retiring the diminutive, 5-foot-11 Trae Young. Dyson Daniels, Jaylen Johnson, Onyeka Okongwu, Zachary Risashe, Nickeil “Shai Gilgeous-Alexander” Alexander-Walker—they’re the very type I’m talking about in the title of this section.

They can score from under the basket, shoot threes (except Daniels…), and on defense, with their long arms, they can snag an extra deflection or steal, and they’re always up for a block.

Even point guards these days don’t measure up to Stockton and Paul. Doncic, Shay, Bol, and even Daniels are all two meters tall. The promising Egor Demin is the size of some centers; according to various sources, he’s between 203 and 207 centimeters.

And so, Atlanta’s expulsion of Young in exchange for 83-year-old CJ McCollum and niche brick maker Corey Kispert (in reality, in exchange for that very same Payroll Relief, which in this situation is far more important than Kispert’s grandmaster-like 36.7% shooting from beyond the 3-point line) is a textbook indicator of exactly which way the wind is blowing in the heads of the current NBA general managers.

Boston, for example, after benching Pritchard, is playing without a pure point guard—and it’s working. Cleveland compensates for the Harden-Mitchell duo with two towers in Mobley and Allen (actually, three, because they also have Dean Wade).

The era of small players who shine on offense and are completely useless on defense seems to be over. Teams aren’t looking for the next Curry anymore; they’re looking for the next Shay, or at the very least, some Amen-Osar Thompson (as if those weren’t enough already).
