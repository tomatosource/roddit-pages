Evaluating Pitching Careers with Leveraged WAR | Who can lay claim to the best season ever by a reliever?

After debuting Leveraged WAR in the context of the Cy Young Award, I felt compelled to share further data on it outside of this context. That primer goes into detail about how the metric was derived, but the gist is that it blends Wins Above Replacement (WAR) and Win Probability Added (WPA) to reflect what voters for the premiere pitching award used to value. It’s purposefully powered only by “advanced” stats to see how those vintage sportswriters might have used them had they had them at their disposal—instead of just wins, saves, and the like.

In that sense, LWAR is designed to reward relief pitchers more than modern sabermetrics suggests they should be. This is because voters in the 70s and 80s put a lot of stock into the save, a stat created by MLB’s former official historian and renowned sportswriter Jerome Holtzmann in 1959. Only until 1969 did the save become a recognized statistic by the league, meaning it was all the rage by the 70s. Although baseball was still in the middle innings when it came to the adoption of the modern closer, players who racked up saves quickly became a hot commodity.

LWAR tries to see how the values behind these trends would have materialized in the modern day had they not been reversed twenty years ago. Though as we will see, the stat may not be so outdated for other accolades. So, let’s look at LWAR in the context of pitching careers as a whole.

This is a fairly unassuming ranking of the greatest pitchers of the last half-century, but the placement of Rivera perks the ears to the stat’s reliever-centric nature. By fWAR alone, the only unanimous Hall of Famer ranks between Derek Lowe and Bob Welch, barely top 75. But in WPA, Rivera ranks 3rd between Maddux and Johnson.

Given the degree to which LWAR benefits relievers via WPA, it seems the stat does a better job at reflecting Hall of Fame sentiment than WAR or WPA alone. Rivera serves as a prime example here, with most certainly agreeing the strength of his candidacy is closer to Scherzer/Halladay/Greinke territory than Lowe/Welch territory. Similarly, the candidacy of Goose Gossage—another Hall of Fame reliever—is probably better represented near the likes of Dave Stieb and Don Sutton (as he is by LWAR) than Aaron Harang and Johnny Cueto (as he is by WAR).

WAR (more specifically JAWS) is the standard for sabermetric Hall of Fame stats, but its downside is that it lacks cross-positional utility among pitchers. LWAR seems to bridge the gap between starters and relievers much more eloquently. That’s not to say LWAR is a better pure value metric than WAR, but I would argue it is a superior Hall of Fame value metric. This is because—just like writers of yesteryear supported relievers for the Cy Young—writers of today support them for the Hall more than WAR suggests they should.

Willie Hernández

Willie Hernández’s inspiring 1984 campaign (that ended in a championship for his Tigers) was the impetus for this whole project due to his unparalleled 8.58 WPA that year. No reliever since has come within 1 WPA of this total, helping his Cy Young victory make more sense in light of his comparatively pedestrian 3.2 WAR.

Of course, Hernández benefits in this metric due to the volume of innings he accrued. Times were different for relievers back then, with the best routinely eclipsing 100 innings out of the bullpen (something that hasn’t been done in about two decades). Hernández in ‘84—as well as Gossage in ‘75 and Corbett in ‘80—pitched over 135 innings. Meanwhile, the next-highest LWAR season—Gagné’s ‘03—was accomplished in only 82.1 innings. Britton in ‘16 is the other LWAR leader in the top 20, and he accomplished that in even less (67.0).

Zack Britton

This time it’s Britton’s clinical 0.54-ERA ‘16 that stands alone. Hernández’s ‘84 is only about 100th all-time on a rate basis, though it’s still top 5 among relief seasons pre-1990. Naturally, this list favors modern closers since their roles became more specialized over time. To put into perspective how impressive these seasons are on a rate basis, the highest LWAR season in the last half-century (Dwight Gooden’s ‘85 CY campaign) produced “only” 3.4 LWAR/100.

Éric Gagné

We again have a different leader who clears the pack this time, as Gagné’s 55-save CY-winning ‘03 campaign may end up being the last time a reliever ever takes home the award. His two seasons surrounding that are also top 20 here; coincidentally, they all saw him pitch exactly 82.1 innings.

Each of LWAR, LWAR/100, and LAWS has its merits as a tool to judge reliever seasons. Although cumulative LWAR was the version designed to be most representative of Cy Young values, it is interesting to think about which of the three seasons that topped each stat (Willie ‘84, Zack ‘16, and Éric ‘03) would win the award if they all occurred in the same season.

Non-closer Relievers by LWAR

Something odd we came across in the original post was Rafael Betancourt being the American League LWAR champion in 2007, edging J.J. Putz and CC Sabathia. The peculiarity stemmed from the fact that Betancourt was not Cleveland’s closer that year, meaning his season represents the only time neither a starter nor a closer was LWAR champion.

LWAR being agnostic to the human-made designations of wins and saves makes sense, but that doesn’t mean this result is any less unrealistic. Betancourt would never win the Cy Young that year in any universe, as the role of the setup pitcher has never carried the requisite inertia to do so. Putz (only a tenth of a point behind him) earned 40 saves that year to Betancourt’s 3, so if a reliever were to be chosen it would have been him. In any case, Sabathia won it, with no relievers finishing top 5 in voting.

Non-closer relievers scoring so highly in LWAR is a treat to me, since that means they were not only elite, but also a) not trusted by their team to be “the guy” yet, and b) still achieved impressive WPA totals despite rarely pitching in the inning where WPA is most volatile (the 9th). In other words, they were unexpectedly good in multiple ways.

Eichhorn benefited from over 150 innings of work in ‘86^(3), so his LWAR/100 isn’t as impressive as others on this list. Also, Rivera’s ‘96 (his best ever LWAR season) is humorous considering his story as the greatest closer ever would begin the very next season.

The highest career LWAR amongst non-closers (defined as relievers who averaged less than 0.2 saves per inning) belongs to Arthur Rhodes (16.4), whose over 20-year career saw him never reach double-digit saves in a season. Despite the occasional mystique of the role, this pretty much proves that non-closer relievers will forever struggle to sniff the Hall of Fame.

Conclusion

This piece was meant to be little more than a listicle for my retro CY sabermetric. It’s not like I was going to break any ground on the “greatest reliever ever” debate, but perhaps I could offer worthwhile insight on the “greatest reliever season ever” one.

Looking forward, I may be drawn further to how LWAR relates to the Hall of Fame, specifically if it might predict induction to a meaningful degree. I may even try applying it to position players—although it was not designed for them, so who knows if it would make much sense.

As Hall of Fame season approaches, further content in that vein is sure to come!
