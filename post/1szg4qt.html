Bad basketball habits in the Jays era

I asked Claude the following question, to which it spit out some interesting points. Curious to hear others’ thoughts on this:

My question: A style of basketball has plagued the Boston Celtics throughout this current “Jays” era. With the exception of their championship season, every year without fail the team succumbs to bad basketball habits: hero ball, shooting ill advised shots, and a “my turn, your turn” style of play. Pure team basketball disappears during spells of games. What are the likely causes of this?

Claude: This is a really interesting structural question about team identity and performance consistency. Here are the most likely causes:

The Dual-Alpha Problem

Jayson Tatum and Jaylen Brown are both wired as primary options — shot creators who were developed individually to carry offensive loads. When the offense stalls, both revert to their default setting: isolation ball. Neither has the passing instincts of a true point-of-attack creator, so instead of moving the ball to reset the offense, they hold it. This is deeply ingrained and hard to coach out of players at this level.

No Natural Pressure Valve

Great team-ball systems typically have a true playmaker who can break down defenses and find shooters when the offense gets stuck — a point guard who controls tempo. The Celtics haven’t had that consistently in the Jays era. Without someone who can reliably make that next pass, the logical outcome in tough moments is hero ball.

Comfort Zone Under Pressure

Playoff basketball and late-game situations trigger regression to what players trust most. For Tatum especially, that’s often the mid-range pull-up or isolation — shots he’s taken thousands of times in development. The system dissolves when the margin for error shrinks because individual confidence in the system is shallower than individual confidence in themselves.

Personality and Accountability Dynamics

There’s been a persistent undercurrent of ambiguity in the Tatum-Brown partnership — subtle tension about hierarchy, shot distribution, and credit. That dynamic, even when managed well, creates a subtle undercutting of genuine selflessness. True team basketball requires subordinating individual ego completely, and two co-equals with overlapping skill sets makes that harder culturally.

Coaching and Habit Reinforcement

Ime Udoka ran a disciplined system that got them to the Finals in 2022. Joe Mazzulla has been more permissive at times with offensive structure, which can allow bad habits to creep back in. Teams often play hero ball because it’s been tolerated — they win enough regular season games through individual talent that the bad habits don’t get corrected until they’re fully exposed in a playoff series.

The Championship Season as the Exception, Not the Proof

2023-24 was notable because depth, health, and a genuine collective buy-in coincided. Kristaps Porzingis gave them a different offensive dimension, Jrue Holiday’s professional culture was infectious, and the role players hit shots consistently enough to reward ball movement. When those conditions weren’t present in other years, the scaffolding came off and the underlying habits returned.

The core issue is essentially this: system basketball is additive, but hero ball is each player’s individual floor. When things get hard, talented players default to what they know. Breaking that cycle requires either a different personnel construction or a level of mental discipline and trust that has proven genuinely difficult to sustain across 82 games and deep playoff runs simultaneously.
