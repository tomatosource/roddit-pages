In pursuit of a baseball game clip I’ve been seeking for almost twenty years…

TL;DR - Andy Pettitte once got a standing ovation for getting thrown out trying to stretch a double into a triple. I really wish I could find video of the event.

Boxscore of the game here.

The date was June 3, 2006, and my dad and I headed to Minute Maid Park in Houston, Texas, to watch the Houston Astros take on the Cincinnati Reds.

The starting pitcher for the Astros was Andy Pettitte, who had mostly struggled through the first couple of months of the season, to the tune of a 5.65 ERA. He was coming off a quality start in a win against the Cardinals, but was also pitching on four days’ rest.

The game was scoreless going into the bottom of the second inning. Being the good old days of the Astros in the National League playing by pre-2022 NL rules, Pettitte came up to bat. He worked the count full, and the fielders came in, anticipating at most a weak grounder.

Then, on Bronson Arroyo’s ninth pitch of the at-bat, chaos ensued.

Pettitte smoked a line drive that soared over Ken Griffey, Jr.’s head. The ball bounced and kept rolling, with enough inertia to keep going up Tal’s Hill in center field… where it rolled clear to the deep center field wall… and stopped.

An incredulous Griffey took off like a bandit to make it up the incline and find the baseball, which was live and in play. And after a half second of shock, the crowd realized what was happening, and we leapt to our feet, roaring with a ferocity usually reserved for October.

Andy Pettitte ran.

As Griffey sped toward the hill and the rest of Reds looked at one another in sheer befuddlement, Pettitte rounded first. Griffey still hadn’t quite gotten his hands on the baseball as he approached second, then turned without hesitation. Third base coach Doug Mansolino was furiously waving his arm, and the crowd screamed and clapped as Pettitte set his sights on third base.

If any fast baserunner had managed to land a ball in that spot, it would’ve been an inside-the-park homer standing up, easily. Even an average baserunner would’ve made it to the third without much drama, and been at least tempted to break for home, even with a legendary centerfielder chasing down the ball.

But soon after making that turn, everyone was reminded that Pettitte had spent most of his career as an American League pitcher. While he continued to will himself forward, his speed took a dramatic dip and his movements became noticeably more labored. Meanwhile, Griffey had finally found the ball.

As Pettitte sputtered and scuffled to make it an even 270 feet, Griffey’s throw from Tal’s Hill made it to shortstop Felipe Lopez, who relayed the ball to third baseman Edwin Encarnacion. We continued to scream and yell, but solely with our hearts, for it had become obvious what was about to happen. Pettitte slid and made it close… but, like, not that close. He was clearly out.

He slowly pulled himself up out of the dirt, and the hometown crowd gave him a hearty and genuinely affectionate ovation. But worst of all… it was the third out of the inning. Pettitte had to immediately go grab his mitt from the dugout and head back to the mound.

Andy Pettitte ended up surrendering seven runs in six innings that game, which the Astros lost to the Reds 7-5. I’m not going to say they definitely lost because Pettitte was worn out from all that running… but I think it’s safe to say it definitely had some kind of impact.

I have searched and scoured the web for a video replay of this event, to no avail. I did find the game recap from the Houston Chronicle, which has some colorful quotes and descriptions. When asked about hitting his double, Pettitte replied, “It’s just stupid… I lost my mind. Running around the bases I guess I lost my mind. I wasn’t able to enjoy it after going out there and pitching the way I did.” (To which my response is to say, hey Andy, when you’ve accomplished as much as you did over the course of your career, you’re allowed to indulge yourself every once in a while!)
