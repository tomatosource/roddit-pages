Paul George is not a Brown replacement. He looks more like a veteran stabilizer.

I do not really see Paul George as a Jaylen Brown replacement.
Brown gave Boston downhill pressure, transition force, physical drives, and a kind of athletic rim pressure that George probably cannot fully replace at this stage of his career. If the question is whether George can copy that role, I think the answer is probably no.
But I also do not think that is the only way to look at the fit.
After watching some of his games, the more interesting question to me is what parts of George’s remaining game can still fit into Boston’s structure.
The cleanest part is the shooting. Last season, George took 4.5 catch-and-shoot threes per game in both the regular season and playoffs, and shot 41.7% and 40.8% on those looks. On threes with a touch time under two seconds, he was also over 40% in both samples.
That matters for Boston. Around Tatum, White, and Pritchard, he does not need to dominate the ball to help. He can space the floor, punish help, and give the offense another big wing who can make quick decisions.
The on-ball value is still there too, but it is not the same as real downhill pressure. He had 5.7 drives per game in the regular season and 4.9 in the playoffs, but in the playoffs those drives only produced 2.2 FGA and 0.5 FTA per game. His playoff drive pass rate was 40.7%.
That matches the film for me. He is not consistently blowing by defenders anymore, but he can still attack closeouts, pull help, and move the ball to the next spot. That is secondary processing, not primary creation.
The passing data fits that connector role as well. In the playoffs, he passed to Maxey 19.4 times per game and to Embiid 6.5 times per game. In the regular season, those numbers were 14.0 and 6.1. That does not make him a point forward, but it does show that he was involved in keeping the ball moving between the main options.
That was one of the things I noticed most on film. George still scans the floor like a veteran. He finds safe outlet spots, relocates into passing windows, points, communicates, and helps possessions keep their shape. Sometimes he reminds me of a soccer midfielder: not always making the final play, but constantly connecting areas of the floor and giving teammates an easier next decision.
Defensively, I would not call him a full-time stopper anymore. The regular-season tracking data was not great. But the playoff sample was better, with opponents shooting 6.1 points worse overall and 8.5 points worse inside 10 feet when defended by him.
I would not overstate those numbers, but they match the film in one way: the awareness is still there. His body does not always keep up with quicker players, and he can still get caught on screens, but the length, positioning, weak-side reads, and communication still matter. It feels more like team defense than lockdown defense now.
That is also where the veteran value matters. If Boston has more young or changing role players, someone like George can help with the small things: spacing correctly, pointing out matchups, knowing when to slow a possession down, knowing where the next pass should go, and helping younger players read the game faster.
So the role is everything.
If Boston needs George to replace Brown’s rim pressure or become a primary creator, that feels dangerous. The burst is not the same, and the rim pressure is not really there anymore.
But if the role is low-touch shooting, spacing, attacking closeouts, secondary processing, safe outlets, team defense, and veteran guidance, I think he can still help.
He is not the explosive answer Brown was. He is not going to raise the ceiling by himself.
But used correctly, he can still be a veteran stabilizer inside Boston’s machine.
