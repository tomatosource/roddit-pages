An under-told NBA story: Seven years after retirement, 41-year-old Bob Cousy made one of the least successful sporting comeback attempts ever, in the process running two NBA top 75 all-time players (Oscar Robertson and Jerry Lucas) out of Cincinnati, and contributing to the city losing its franchise

Bob Cousy retired still an all-star and champion in 1963, the greatest point guard to ever play the game at the time. Seven years later, he made what has to be one of the least successful non-boxing sporting comebacks ever, now on the Cincinnati Royals. In seven games, the 41-year-old Cousy averaged .7 points and 1.4 assists as a player-coach, but his complete impact was much more devastating.

The year is 1969. The NBA has only 14 teams, the Atlanta Hawks are still in the Western Conference, and the Boston Celtics have just won their 9th championship in 10 years.

Six-time NBA champion and 13-time all-star Bob Cousy has freshly departed from his six-year gig coaching Boston College to join the Cincinnati Royals, who had just missed the playoffs due to playing in a stacked Eastern Conference with the Earl “The Pearl” Monroe Bullets, Willis Reed Knicks, and a dominant Celtics team on their last legs.

The Royals at the time were led by the ‘Big O’ Oscar Robertson and Jerry Lucas. Oscar is known as an all-time great, Lucas was 3x All-NBA first team and 2x All-NBA second team from 64-68, and might have made an all-decade team.

While Robertson was beginning to slow down due to age years removed from his MVP title, he was still a dominant force on the court and was the league leader in assists for 7 out of the last 9 years, having averaged 24.7⁄6.4⁄9.8 in the season prior with teammate Tom van Arsdale lessening the offensive burden placed upon him.

At the time, he was the successor to Cousy as the most talented point guard ever, he just didn’t have rings to prove it.

It was clear that the teams to beat going into the 1969-1970 season were the Los Angeles Lakers with their trinity of Elgin Baylor, Jerry West, and Wilt Chamberlain, followed by the New York Knicks with their 6 players averaging over 15ppg.

Having attained a legendary new head coach in Bob Cousy to put alongside the improving Jerry Lucas & Tom van Arsdale, perhaps it would be possible for the Robertson and the Royals to sneak in as a dark horse. Right out the gate, on October 15 1969, the Royals’s first match was against the Knicks.

The game was fairly standard. Robertson shot a rough 8⁄26 while Lucas shot 6⁄10, losing 89-94 to the Knicks, though not before a shoving match erupted between top-75 player Dave DeBusschere and Tom van Arsdale, with Bob Cousy doing his best to ease tensions by giving his player the sage advice to “take his (DeBusschere’s) head clean off” the next time he tried anything, to which the Knick responded by taunting him to “come out of retirement and try it”.

That might have landed with Bob. Before coming out of retirement, Cousy dashed the hopes of the people of Cincinnati by trading away future HoF/top-75 player Jerry Lucas just four games into the season to the San Fransisco Warriors in return for Jim King and Bill Turner. Upon being traded to the Royals, Jim went from averaging 16.3ppg to 2.9pgg due to knee issues, which would force him to retire on the Chicago Bulls just three years later. Bill Turner would average 7.2ppg triple-single. Neither of these two players would start the next season in a Royals jersey.

As fate would have it, the Royals (9-11) and Knicks (22-1) would face off again on November 28 in Cleveland, with an interesting caveat: the Knicks were a single win away from breaking the NBA record for wins in a row at the time, a record which was once held by Cousy’s 1960 Celtics at 17 wins.

This time however, Robertson was ready. Putting on a stunning 33-point performance on 15⁄23 shots made with 10 assists, the Royals were leading by 3 points with 1:49 remaining on the clock when he fouled out. Perhaps believing this to be a message from God, the 41-year-old Coach Cousy decided that this was the perfect moment to show these young Eastern Conference upstarts how it’s done and defend the glorious Celtics’s legacy. He subbed himself in and sank two free throws after getting fouled.

With 16 seconds left, they fouled Willis Reed, who sank his own free throws. Legacy on the line, Cousy decided the best course of action was run the plays through himself. With ten seconds to go, it was fairly certain the Royals were going to win. After all, there was no 3-point line.

However, that all changed when the 6-time NBA champion Bob Cousy, now 41 and slow in body and processing time, decided to turn over the ball twice, leading to DeBusschere and Frazier sealing the deal. Final score, 106-105. Knicks win. A new record was set in the NBA, with 18 wins in a row. They would then go on to become the 1970 NBA champions, as the Royals sat at home with a 36-46 record, 5 games worse than the previous year.

With the disappointing season behind them, Cousy decided that the best course of action was to finally put Royals fans out of their misery by getting rid of perennial all-star Oscar Robertson, just as he had with his co-star Jerry Lucas. The return was exceptionally bad, getting Charlie Paulk and Flynn Robinson, two players who would be disposed of in a year.

Over the years, Robertson has had some pretty interesting remarks regarding the subject:

“He was in his forties, hadn’t played in six years, and he put himself in-can you imagine that?”

“He put himself in because he didn’t trust anyone else, like he was trying to make a point: ‘I’ll show you how it’s done.’”

“Everyone knows you make the shortest pass and hold the ball, but what does he do? He puts the ball in the air.”

“No alarm, no criticism in the media about how a guy at that age could do that.”

On getting traded: “I think he was wrong and I will never forget it.”

Despite this however, getting traded by Bob Cousy might’ve been one of the best things to happen to these players. Robertson would go on to win the 1971 NBA championship alongside sophomore Lew Alcindor on the Milwaukee Bucks, while Jerry Lucas would join the Knicks on their 1973 title run, feats that certainly wouldn’t have been possible had they stayed on the Royals.

Cincinnati’s franchise continued to struggle throughout the Cousy years, and by 1972 the team relocated to Kansas City as a result of declining fan attendance from the Oscar Robertson years. In five years coaching the team, he never had a winning record.

Cousy later said about the job in Cincinnati, “I did it for the money. I was made an offer I couldn’t refuse.”
