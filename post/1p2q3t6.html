What do pitchers mean when they say they pitch differently depending on who’s batting next?

I’ve long been stumped by what this means and what the exact execution of “pitching around a protected batter” would look like. I’ve heard so many players and analysts talk about lineup protection and how, depending on whether the guy hitting behind you is good or bad, pitchers will pitch differently to you. I sort of understand the idea, but I’m clueless on how that would even be implemented, or if it’s even true at all.

Stats + Jimmy Rollins interview:

According to this blog post that crunched the numbers, protected batters only saw a 0.25% increase in strikes, and a 0.07% increase in pitches over the middle of the plate, compared to unprotected batters, which translates to an additional 6 strikes and 2 pitches down the middle over the course of an entire season for a protected batter. If we were to apply a P-value to this, it would be nowhere near significant, and it is almost a 100% chance that the difference is purely luck.

In addition, Jimmy Rollins stated in an interview that “If you’ve been around, and you’ve seen the game, you can’t argue the fact that the guy behind you, if he’s a good hitter, if he’s a bad hitter, it’s going to dictate what you see. If he’s bad, you think I’m giving in to you? Here, hit this. If you get on, and there’s two outs, I can get him out. Or, ‘Whoo, oh yeah, I see that guy.’ Then they do everything they can to get you out. It’s like saying I saw eight pitches”, which basically just says lineup protection does nothing to determine how a batter will get pitched to because, no matter what, your goal will never be to give a free pass, it will always be to get the hitter out (given that the batter doesn’t receive an IBB).

So according to what I’ve seen and read, the numbers don’t support the idea of lineup protection being a determinant for pitching strategy at all, and one of the greatest shortstops of his era seems to agree. The only thing that I can think of that could potentially change based on batting order is specific tunneling sequences. For example, as a pitcher, if you have 2 or 3 pitches that tunnel really well together and you’ve figured out a sequence that is insanely effective for you, then you’d probably use the 2nd or 3rd best sequence you have on the weaker batter and save the best ones for the stronger batter, especially since the next batter is always watching and analyzing from the on deck circle.

Of course, this is just from my own perspective and personal opinion, so I would love to hear different or opposing thoughts and any evidence that supports or refutes my claim!
