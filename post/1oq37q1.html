PSA for the upcoming Free Agency: Deferrals do not give any team any sort of advantage in issuing contracts. The only parties affected by a deferred contract are the player and the state they would be paying income taxes in. Us fans can pretty much ignore deferral amounts entirely.

I feel like every year some player signs a big contract with a big chunk of it deferred, and fans start losing their minds thinking that some financial accounting voodoo is going on that magically saves the team a bunch of money because the contract is deferred. That is not how it works at all. The media holds a lot of the blame because they report on deferred amounts, even though the deferred amount is entirely irrelevant to us fans and to the teams. Here is a basic breakdown of how deferrals work:

Ohtani signs a 10-year, \(700M contract. However \)680M of those payments are deferred for 10+ years. That means that the true Present Value of the contract is actually \(460M. How is this calculated? The MLB has it written into the CBA to use a 5% annual interest rate for calculating the Present Value of deferred contracts. **The Present Value is what counts towards the CBT and the true amount of wealth the player is gaining.** So the Dodgers **did** pay \)70M (in 2034 dollars) to Ohtani in 2024. They paid Ohtani \(2M directly, and they set side \)44M (\(68M in 2034 dollars) in escrow - which (at 5% annual interest) will accumulate to \)68M by 2034. That repeats every year.

To put it another way - imagine if today someone offered you \(1,000. Would you expect that \)1,000 to be worth the same amount 10 years from now? No of course not, inflation (and other factors) exist. So if someone said “I am going to offer you \(1,000 - but I am not going to pay you for 10 years." Wouldn't you demand you be paid *more* than \)1,000 in 10 years?

Why do teams/players do this? Teams do it because it makes the player happy - that’s it. It doesn’t affect the team in any real way other than that. Players do it to save money on state taxes. In Ohtani’s case, he is working in the state of California which has a top marginal tax rate of 13.3%. However if his income payments are deferred for at least 10 years, then he pays the taxes in the state he lives in at the time he receives those payments. This is a federal law in the United States. So if Ohtani moves to an income tax-free state like Texas or Washington (or back to Japan) after his 10 year deal, he will be receiving those \(68M payments without paying 13.3% of it to the state of California. Which means Ohtani stands to gain about \)60M (in 2024 dollars) by choosing to have his contract deferred (and the state of California loses out on that money).

Jeff Passan once explained all of this in a Tweet:

I’m going to explain why Shohei Ohtani’s \(700 million contract with the Los Angeles Dodgers will not equal \)700 million in terms of MLB accounting or the present-day value of the deal.

When money in a contract is deferred, the competitive-balance tax number — the luxury tax — is discounted. With a source saying a “majority” of Ohtani’s contract is deferred, the discount could be significant. Typically, a CBT number is the average annual value of a deal — in this case \(70 million. But depending on the size and length of the deferrals, Ohtani’s CBT number is likelier to wind up in the \)40-50 million-a-year range, an enormous benefit for the Dodgers.

The deferrals also affect the net present value of the deal. There’s a rule of thumb across all walks of life: Money today is more valuable than money tomorrow, inflation being what it is. When you defer money, you’re taking less.
