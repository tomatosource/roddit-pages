Extra Innings: A Dinger x Mrs. Met fanfiction. Chapter 1.

The thing about being Mrs. Met was that everyone assumed she was always smiling. The truth was, the smile never came off.

It was stitched into her face.

Perfect, permanent, and useless when the crowd had gone home and the lights at Citi Field dimmed to a low, lonely glow. The echo of cheers lingered in the concrete corridors like ghosts that didn’t know they were dead.

Mrs. Met sat alone on the edge of the dugout bench, her oversized blue sneakers dangling just above the dirt. Her hands rested in her lap, white gloves folded neatly, as if she were still on display. Even now, she kept her posture immaculate. Years of habit. Another home loss. Another polite wave to the crowd. Another night walking back to the locker room beside Mr. Met without either of them saying anything that mattered.

She glanced down at the diamond. Groundskeepers were already at work, smoothing the infield, erasing the evidence that anything emotional had happened here at all. That was how baseball worked. Everything reset by morning.

Her phone buzzed.

She hesitated before checking it. Mascots weren’t supposed to be distracted. Weren’t supposed to have phones, really. But she did. Hidden inside the pocket sewn into her costume, just above the hip.

Unknown Contact

Long night?

Her thumb hovered. Then she typed back.

Always.

The typing bubbles appeared almost immediately.

Same. Coors feels extra big when it’s empty.

She smiled. A real smile. The one no one ever saw.

Dinger had that effect on her.

They’d met six months earlier in Seattle, of all places.

An interleague promotional event. Mascots from across the league crammed into one hotel ballroom, tripping over oversized feet and laughing too loud because that’s what was expected.

Mrs. Met hadn’t planned on talking to anyone beyond the required photo ops.

Dinger hadn’t planned on talking to anyone at all.

He’d been standing near the refreshment table, tail awkwardly curled behind him, staring at a plate of cookies like it might personally betray him.

“You know those are free, right?” she’d said.

He’d jumped. Six inches straight up.

She apologized immediately, even though it was funny.

Especially because it was funny.

They talked. About nothing. About everything. About how strange it was to be beloved by millions and known by none. About the loneliness that came with being a symbol instead of a person. She didn’t even realize how late it had gotten until hotel staff started stacking chairs.

Since then, it had been texts. Calls. Messages sent at 2 a.m. from different time zones, when neither of them could sleep because the roar of the crowd still rang in their heads.

Back in Queens, Mrs. Met stood and brushed dirt from her knees, even though that dirt was purposely put on there in the first place. Mr. Met was already gone. She noticed that more and more lately.

Thousands of miles away, Dinger sat in the upper deck at Coors Field, legs stretched out, tail resting beside him. Denver’s skyline glimmered beyond the outfield wall, indifferent and beautiful. He reread her last message.

Always.

He typed, erased, typed again.

You ever feel like the inning never ends?

She took longer to reply this time.

Every day.

Dinger leaned back and stared at the stars. He wondered what it would be like to sit next to her instead of talking through a screen. To hear her laugh without crowd noise swallowing it whole. He wondered when exactly friendship had started feeling like something else.

He wondered if she wondered too.

By design, Mrs. Met was never alone.

Even when she walked the service tunnels beneath the stadium, security cameras followed her. Even when she sat in the locker room, costume half-off, sweat cooling against her skin, there was always the knowledge that she existed for someone else.

She unzipped the oversized head and set it carefully on the bench, facing forward, eyes wide and eternally cheerful. The silence that followed felt obscene, like she’d done something wrong just by removing it.

Across the room, Mr. Met was already changing. They moved around each other with the choreography of a couple who had long ago learned how not to collide. Parallel lines that never quite touched.

“Crowd was decent,” he said eventually, voice muffled as he tugged his jersey free.

“Mm,” she replied.

That was it. That was the conversation.

She watched him in the mirror as she peeled the rest of the costume away. Without the uniform, he looked tired in a way that scared her.

They hadn’t always been like this. She knew that. She remembered laughing together once, years ago, when everything still felt new and ridiculous and hopeful. But memory was a dangerous thing. It had a way of exaggerating warmth that might never have truly existed.

He left first, as usual. The door closed with a soft click. Mrs. Met sat alone on the bench and pressed her palms into her eyes until the pressure hurt.

Her phone buzzed.

She didn’t look at it right away. Because she already knew who it was.

In Denver, Dinger lay flat on his back in the dark, the ceiling fan chopping the air above him into uneven pieces. His phone rested on his chest, screen lit.

You okay? he’d sent.

Three dots appeared. Disappeared. Appeared again.

Yeah, she finally wrote. Just tired.

Dinger rolled onto his side and stared at the wall. His apartment was quiet in a way that made noise feel intrusive. The only thing that got in was the low hum of the city outside, living its life without him.

He typed slowly.

You don’t have to pretend with me.

There was a longer pause this time.

Long enough that he wondered if he’d crossed a line.

I don’t know how not to, she replied.

His chest tightened. That was the problem, wasn’t it? They were both experts at performance.

Mrs. Met lay awake long after the lights went out in the apartment she shared with her husband. Mr. Met’s breathing was steady beside her, deep and untroubled. She wondered if he dreamed. She wondered if she ever did anymore.

Her phone buzzed again beneath the pillow.

Do you ever feel like you’re cheating, he typed, even when you haven’t done anything?

The reply came almost instantly.

All the time.

She turned onto her side, facing the wall, tears leaking quietly into the pillowcase.

I don’t want to be this person, she wrote. I don’t want to be someone who wants something she’s not supposed to.

On the other end of the country, Dinger sat up in bed, phone clutched tight.

What do you want?

She stared at the question until the words blurred. The honest answer scared her too much to send. Instead, she wrote:

Peace.

It wasn’t a lie. It just wasn’t the whole truth.

The next week was a road trip for the Mets, and she was on tour.

Hotels, buses, the same jokes told by the same people in different cities.

Mrs. Met smiled. Danced. Threw t-shirts into the stands. Did everything right. Inside, she was unraveling thread by thread.

She and Dinger talked less, not because either of them wanted to, but because the spaces between messages grew heavier.

Every text carried weight now. Every word felt like a step closer to something neither of them had agreed to name.

One night, after a late game in St. Louis, she sat on the edge of the hotel bathtub, costume hanging limp from a hook like a discarded skin.

Her phone rang. She answered without thinking.

“Hey,” she said softly.

Dinger exhaled on the other end, relief obvious. “Hey.”

They stayed quiet for a moment, just breathing, just existing together across the distance.

“I almost didn’t call,” he admitted.

“Why did you?” she asked.

“Because every time I don’t talk to you, it feels like I’m disappearing.”

Her throat closed. She pressed her forehead against the cool tile. “I’m married,” she said suddenly.

“I know.”

“I love him,” she said, and hated how uncertain it sounded.

“I know that too.”

Silence again. Thicker this time. “I don’t want to be the thing that breaks you,” Dinger said quietly.

Mrs. Met closed her eyes. “Neither do I.”

And yet.

Back home in Queens, Mr. Met noticed the change before she did. “You’re distracted,” he said one evening as they sat at opposite ends of the couch.

She didn’t look up from her phone. “Busy schedule.”

He nodded, accepting the answer the way he always did.

That was the most damning part. She wanted him to fight. To accuse. To care enough to make this hard. Instead, he turned back to the television, and she felt something inside her collapse under the weight of being unchallenged.

That night, she texted Dinger from the bathroom floor.

I think the worst part is that no one would stop me.

His reply came after a long pause.

I would.

She cried then. Fully.

Because she knew he meant it.
