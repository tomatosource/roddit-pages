The Monstars in 2025

The infamous squad in *Space Jam* was created by stealing the basketball powers of Muggsy Bogues, Charles Barkley, Larry Johnson, Patrick Ewing, and Shawn Bradley. A formidable squad 30 years ago, they did end up losing to Michael Jordan and the Tune squad (perhaps because of their lack of depth - Bradley’s alien didn’t record a single stat) on a buzzer beater. A crushing defeat, we never got to see those five players play with each other again. But, with the power of imagination, we can. For our thought experiment, let’s pretend the five NBA players (not aliens) took a time machine to 2025 after they regained their powers. How would the expansion Seattle Supersonics do if they signed the five time travelers, hired a coach, filled out the team, and played a full NBA schedule?

In order to answer that question, we should first figure out how good they were when the movie took place. And, the first step to that is to figure out *when* the movie took place.

In separate scenes, you see Michael Jordan playing a professional baseball game and the NBA players playing in professional basketball games. Their seasons only overlap in the spring/April. Jordan only played baseball for one year, so it would seem the movie takes place in April of 1994 (i.e. the end of the 1993-94 season). However, at the end of the movie, you see Jordan lace it up for the Bulls, and he didn’t make his return to the NBA until March of 1995, so the movie would have to take place in the 1994-95 season. Further still, the Knicks-Suns game takes place on November 7th, 1995 which would mean the movie takes place in the 1995-96 season. There is not a clear-cut answer, but I believe the events in the movie taking place in the 1994-95 NBA season makes the most sense.

So, how good were these players in the 1994-95 season?

Utilizing basketball-reference, we can combine PER, WS/48, and BPM into one statistic. From there, multiplying by minutes played and dividing by 48 minutes, we can calculate each player’s wins added. Barkley is the best of the bunch just edging out Ewing despite missing some games. Bradley brings up the rear as he was a still a work in progress in his second year in the NBA. I did look at the season before and after for all five players to see if there was anything bizarre, but I didn’t see anything that necessitates using stats from outside the 94-95 season.

Barkley - 10.35 Wins from 2,383 Min at 0.208 adjWS/48

Ewing - 10.25 Wins from 2,920 Min at 0.169 adjWS/48

Grandmama - 9.10 Wins from 3,234 Min at 0.135 adjWS/48

Bogues - 7.31 Wins from 2,629 Min at 0.134 adjWS/48

Bradley - 4.67 Wins from 2,365 Min at 0.095 adjWS/48

For those doing math at home, those five players would give you a team that should be expected to win 41.7 games. However, that doesn’t include any contributions from the bench - there is still over 6,000 minutes to disperse. But, how effective is the bench in these minutes?

If we use Stathead and look at the 250 players in 1994-95 who weren’t starters (played less than 2,500 minutes and started less than half of their games) we can find the “average” bench player’s PER, WS/48, and BPM. Once again, multiplying by minutes (6,305 by accounting for some overtime games) and dividing by 48, we find the bench should contribute another 11 wins.

So, in 1994-95, the Monstars would be expected to win 52.65 games. Is this reasonable? Could a team with superstars Barkley and Ewing only be the 7th best team in the NBA?

Conviently, we can make a quick comparison to the Charlotte Hornets who rostered two of the Monstars. Looking at the three non-Hornets, we can comp Ewing to Alonzo Mourning. I think Ewing post-peak was a little better than pre-peak Mourning, but it is a similar comparison. Barkley is a clear upgrade over Hershey Hawkins, but Hawkins was no slouch. Then, Bradley doesn’t add much and is actually slightly worse than the Hornets fifth starter, Scott Burrell. Quick, back of notebook estimates, I would say the Ewing swap is +1, Barkley swap is +3 (+4 if he was healthier), and Bradley swap is -1. Looking back, we see the Hornets won 50 games in 94-95 which would give the Monstars-Hornets 53 wins which is right in line with our previous estimate. Of course, we should double check the Hornets win total as well. First, their point differential did produce a 50-win expectation, so they weren’t obscenely lucky (or unlucky) to hit 50 wins. Also, if we do the same calculations as above (combining PER, WS/48, and BPM and multiplying by minutes played), we can see that the Hornets players should combine to a 49-win team. This is to say, the calculations are reasonable.

Of course, saying the Monstars would be a 53-win team in 94-95 is omitting one important aspect - roster synergy. The Monstars don’t really have any wings, in fact, their only backcourt player is 5’3.” That is not ideal construction to say the least, and it is even worse for the modern game…

Translating to 2025

Dean Oliver found the quality of the NBA gets better by about 1% every year. I wouldn’t bet my life on the precision of this estimate, but this suggests that an NBA player taken from 1995 to 2025 would be 74% as valuable (i.e. 1.01^-30). It is important to note a replacement level NBA player has a WS/48 of 0.035 (which is equivalent to a 14-win team). For the five Monstars, this would mean they would project to add 33.4 wins to an NBA team in 2025 compared to 41.7 wins added to a team in 1995. That’s reasonable…? I don’t really know what people would expect. Either way, we should take a closer player-by-player look. Let’s start with Shawn Bradley.

Shawn Bradley

The 7’6” center was an elite rim protector but his offense left a lot to be desired (which is an understatement as it was below replacement for each of his first three seasons). I actually think his offensive numbers would look better/be more efficient now as he was asked to do too much back then. Over his first five years in the NBA, he had a 21% usage rate and a 47% true shooting percentage. Compare that to the next five years, he had a 15% usage rate and a 55% true shooting percentage. Instead of playing in the post and forcing shots and turnovers, he would just be asked to finish lobs and putbacks.

His defense is where I get more concerned (in terms of drop off). He is still going to be able to block and affect shots, but mobility is at more of a premium than it used to be. I think he is a little more mobile than fellow 7’6” big man Tacko Fall, but the modern game will still be tough on him. Still, we’ve seen teams deploy drop coverages to maximize the defense of Brook Lopez and Rudy Gobert, so it is possible. I would also add that there has been a slight resurgence in the ultra-big man as teams are starting to look into the matchup problems they create.

https://preview.redd.it/mv6oypo8f8lf1.png?width=938&format=png&auto=webp&s=0388cf8bd5388458ffb0dbf78b505ca58c2ce32a

In 2022, the only players over 7 feet tall who got 1000 minutes were Rudy Gobert and Kristaps Porzingis. 2025 featured the most 1000-minute ultra-bigs in over 20 years, and that’s not including Chet Holmgren, Dereck Lively, or Jay Huff who all should be healthier/get more run next year. I am notably out on Khaman Maluach, but he joins Donovan Clingan and Zach Edey as a non-unicorn ultra-big lottery picks over the past three years.

Muggsy Bogues

There has not been a similar resurgence of ultra-small guards which doesn’t bode well for the 5’3” Bogues. In the current NBA, he’d likely be the worst defender. This isn’t a death knell in itself - Trae Young can’t play defense but he is an all-star level player because of how good he is offensively. However, 90’s point guards weren’t looking for their shot. Bogues likely won’t be able to put pressure on the defense like Isaiah Thomas or JJ Barea. His best comp would be a pass-first point guard like Facundo Campazzo or Yuki Kawamura. Kawamura was the only player under 6’ in the NBA last year and is on a two-way (i.e. replacement level) for the Chicago Bulls this year. It is hard to project Bogues as having the vision and creativity that Kawamura showed in Summer League.

Charles Barkley and Larry Johnson

The two most common comps for Zion Williamson coming out of college, Barkley and Johnson were mismatches in their day because of their combination of athleticism and power for their size. Johnson had just hurt his back the season before, so he had started to his expand his game outwards a la Pistons Blake Griffin. Barkley was also his past his athletic peak at 31 and was no longer putting up insane shooting efficiency, but he was still an All-NBA player grabbing boards and was at his peak decision-making.

Grandmama I am more confident will be around that 74% conversion rate. His athleticism will translate, and he did show some perimeter skills even if it would be below average nowadays. Meanwhile, I think it is still possible Barkley is a mismatch nightmare, but I am not confident that that carries over to the modern day. He is past his peak athleticism, and the cost-benefit of bullying players down low seems much lower than it used to.

Patrick Ewing

Now, Patrick Chewing I think could punish under-sized centers down low. Like Barkley, he is past his athletic peak but in his passing prime. I think Ewing would be able to do work in the post and have the vision to kick out on doubles. Of course, to punish under-sized centers on offense, he has to guard small-ball on defense. The Knicks had the best defense in 1995, but it was more of a team effort than Ewing being DPOY. Still, he was a good defensive player in 1995, but did he have the mobility to be a good defender in 2025? Maybe not, but maybe I am overrating it since there are still centers like Nikola Vucevic getting 30 mpg.

The other thing I like about Ewing in the modern day is his shooting. Now, he is not going to be a knock-down shooter, but he does have the peripherals to suggest he could add the 3-point shot to his bag. He is a career 74% shooter from the line, and for 1997 on (the years basketball-reference has shot data on), he shot 41% on long two’s. Vucevic is 77⁄44, Pau Gasol is 75⁄43, Al Horford 76⁄46, Julius Randle 75⁄35, and Myles Turner 77⁄44 are some examples of modern players who developed a 3 after they reached the NBA.

Also important to note, Barkley and Grandmama were 74⁄44 and 77⁄38, respectively. It does seem reasonable to assume all three would have a below average 3-point shot in 2025 which should help with spacing.

Synergy

Of course, that is still going to be the worst spacing of any team in the NBA. How important is spacing? It would take some time to run the numbers, but let’s look at some anecdotes. The Cavs this year broke out with improved outside shooting (e.g. Evan Mobley developed his stroke, Ty Jerome leapt over Isaac Okoro in the rotation) after experimenting with ultra-big lineups for a couple years. The Horford-Brand Sixers were the only Sixers team from 2018-2023 to finish with a worse than 60% win-rate. The 2019 Bucks broke out when replacing non-shooter John Henson with shooter (and better player) Brook Lopez.

It is not all bad. There is always value in zigging when others zag. The Monstars would dominate the boards (moreso even than this year’s Rockets), and their offense would be less variant than one designed around threes. Mismatches also go both ways, so they would be a team that modern teams would have to adjust for.

Conclusion

Now, for this thought experiment, I am assuming a competent front office and coaching staff, so the rest of the roster will be guard heavy. Also, the defensive and spacing concerns will be somewhat mitigated by installing a drop defense (to maximize the Ewing/Bradley center combo) and a more modern shot profile. Teams may score 6 more points per 100 possessions nowadays, but half of that is probably shot profile.

If we assume Bradley and Bogues are replacement players, Ewing, Johnson, and Barkley retain 80%, 75%, and 70% of their value, and the spacing issues and transition to the modern game cost them 5 wins, then their expected win total is… 33.3. That puts them 3 wins behind the Suns who still had a couple good/great players in Kevin Durant and Devin Booker, but had poor team construction.

Fortunately, we will never know how this will play out, so we won’t know how wrong I am, but it does seem to be in the ballpark. Before doing any of the math, I thought 45 wins was the Monstars ceiling (if their talent wins out and they just prove to be a mismatch) and 15 wins their floor (if they can’t adjust to the modern game, but they aren’t tanking).
