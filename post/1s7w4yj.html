[Aris Barkas] Negotiations between NBA and EuroLeague to start again soon. Instead of two rival leagues, we may end up with a joint 24-team league.

The end of March marks the conclusion of the bidding process for potential NBA Europe investors. However, after many twists and turns, the future of European basketball may ultimately hinge on a collaboration between all major stakeholders.

Per Eurohoops sources, the EuroLeague and the NBA are expected to meet in the next few days with the goal of finally creating a joint project, even at the 11th hour. While no official announcement has been made, recent statements from NBA Commissioner Adam Silver and EuroLeague CEO Chus Bueno have signaled a clear openness toward a possible collaboration.

Meanwhile, the NBA Europe project remains on track for a 2027 tipoff. The central question now is whether this will result in a joint competition that incorporates the EuroLeague. Sources close to the bidding process indicate that interest in the NBA project is coming primarily from US funds targeting the UK and Rome, alongside local interest in Paris. Due to ongoing conflicts in the Middle East, Arabian funds are currently more reluctant. While there is money on the table, stakeholders generally agree that no party is currently willing to pay a €500M entry fee for the new league.

On the other side, the EuroLeague Board will meet on April 14th to discuss next season’s format. While expansion is a theoretical possibility, the most likely outcome is another season with 20 teams under the current format. The futures of Monaco and ASVEL remain uncertain for different reasons, and adding teams could complicate existing negotiations. The desire for a deal is evident from both sides. The presence of Chus Bueno, a former NBA executive, simplifies negotiations. However, for a deal to materialize, it must be a “win-win” for all involved. In 2024, the NBA’s offer to take 51% control of the EuroLeague stalled talks. Today, with the EuroLeague valued at €3.2B, the landscape has changed. Sources indicate that Real Madrid and Fenerbahce are close to signing new 10-year licenses, solidifying the EuroLeague’s position ahead of these high-level talks.

FIBA, for its part, is seeking a calendar that accommodates national team “windows” and provides a clear qualification path from domestic leagues to the top-tier club competition. If the EuroLeague and the NBA reach a financial agreement, FIBA’s demands are expected to be respected. Under this scenario, a 24-team competition is a possibility to accommodate both established and new clubs.

While it may seem ambitious, there is growing hope that two years of maneuvering will finally end the 26-year rift between FIBA and the EuroLeague, paving the way for a mutually profitable collaboration with the NBA.
