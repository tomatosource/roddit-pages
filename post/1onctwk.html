The Elite Starter Rises Again | Starting pitching in the 2025 postseason returned to a form not seen in October since before the pandemic.

Seemingly everywhere you turn in baseball discussions nowadays, someone will be lamenting the modern starting pitcher. The shift from starters traversing deep into games—often throwing complete games consistently—to the proliferation of relievers out of the bullpen is well-documented, and is usually the primary reason for the lamentations. Less innings per start means more dispersed workloads, leading to a greater struggle for games to form a cohesive narrative. Carried by this desire for more gripping stories, some have supported the idea of a six-inning minimum start rule (with caveats), though this could foment unintended consequences.

The scourge of strikeouts serves as another scapegoat that isn’t exclusive to starters but is still satisfying to scoff at. More strikeouts means less action (as defined by the involvement of fielders), and is thus less entertaining to those seeking that action. A similar trend with home runs^(1) has surfaced lately as well, meaning we’re now fully entrenched in the “Three True Outcomes” era.

The main reason why these shifts have occurred is because teams have realized the importance of pitch quality (“stuff”), and are willing to sacrifice the starter’s workload if it means challenging opposing hitters with richer and more diverse ammunition. Although this general trend is probably never leaving baseball, recent analysis suggests teams may have already reached the inflection point regarding the utility of yanking starters early.

Whether starter nostalgia is borne more out of genuine quality gripes or rosy retrospection will probably remain an eternal question. I doubt it has as much to do with fans’ enjoyment of the game as pace of play, given how much the pitch clock is credited as the primary force for the recent upswing in viewership and attendance. Nevertheless, it is a talking point that has endured throughout the 2025 season and will likely continue to beyond that. But for one month of high-stakes baseball, fans of the old school starter were treated to a bit of a reprieve. In October, elite starting pitching returned with a vengeance.

Last year, the postseason pitching narrative revolved around the decrepitude of most bullpens^(2). To show this, we can use ERA-, which simply places Earned Run Average on an intuitive scale where 100 is average. In the postseason, teams are generally better, which means the pool of pitchers is also, leading to most ERA- numbers being below 100 (i.e., better than average) in October. However, the ERA- of relievers in the 2024 postseason was 94, which is actually the highest it’s been since the similarly notorious 2019, which was itself highest since 2011. This year’s reliever ERA- wasn’t worlds better (87), but is closer to what we would expect given talent levels.

This year, the postseason pitching narrative was the dominant starter. The record-breaking runs total from Toronto was not enough to squelch the trend of better starting pitching this month, with an overall starter ERA- of 91. This won’t seem notable compared to the reliever numbers above, but consider that the average starter ERA- in the Wild Card era (since 2012) is about 98, whereas the average reliever ERA- in that span is about 86. To starter standards, the 2025 postseason was well above average, and served as a breath of fresh air from the previous four postseasons which all featured a starter ERA- of 102-106.

Does this increased quality mean that starters this postseason were also accruing a higher quantity of innings? Not exactly. For every nailed starter who went deeper than their regular season numbers would indicate, there was an opener^(3) who was designed to only throw for an inning or so. This resulted in an average innings per start (IP/GS) of about 4.5, which is in line with the last handful of postseasons. So, the pitching meta is evidently still a far cry from what it once was. We’re not in an “old school” resurgence or anything.

Unless you’re just crazy good, that is. Indeed, the dominant starting pitching this October had less to do with managers putting their foot down about sabermetrics and everything to do with them having no choice but to sit in the passengers seat while their elite starters put the pedal to the metal. Even so, they weren’t so “old school” in their approach, with high strikeout rates abounding (the exceptions were Yamamoto’s complete games, which were uncharacteristic for someone who ranked 42nd out of 47 qualified starters in Old School Pitching in the regular season).

Amusingly, Ohtani’s seemingly facile victory over the Brewers in Game 4 of the NLCS barely misses the top 10% thanks to Yesavage’s timely WS Game 5 masterpiece. When a pitching performance like Ohtani’s can’t make the cut, you know you’re working in rarefied air. The average GmSc of this 90th percentile crop being ~81 signals a return to form for elite starting pitching:

https://preview.redd.it/gwmu6ekku1zf1.jpg?width=1048&format=pjpg&auto=webp&s=0f6944a02f4468159452a7e7f0625291e58231b2

The variation throughout this graph makes sense in the context of the small samples of October baseball, but there are notable trends here. The best 10% of starts (which I’ll call “blue chip starts”) averaged an 80+ GmSc much more frequently around the turn of the century. Blue chip starts took a slight dip in the 00s before 2010’s anomalous excellence, then the rest of the 10s routinely flirted with and sometimes exceeded 80. However, after the pandemic hit, the dip in starter workloads came into full effect, fusing with unremarkable general run prevention to create measly blue chip starts compared to years past. But that trend was swiftly bucked this year.

In honor of a good cherry-pick, limiting the visual to just the 20s clarifies how unique this postseason’s starting pitching was in the post-COVID landscape:

https://preview.redd.it/ch6tqmylu1zf1.jpg?width=610&format=pjpg&auto=webp&s=d1e74240a52271dec015f922327241a7dbb24916

This year appears even more outlandish in the context of workload trends. All but two of the prior 80+ GmSc blue chip start years averaged at least 5.5 IP/GS, with some eclipsing 6.0 (the exceptions were 2016 and 2019, which averaged 5.1). Comparatively, 2025’s 4.5 IP/GS sticks out like a sore thumb. Incredible is the fact that many of the best starters in baseball this fall were able to muster such indelible performances in spite of the commonly short leash nowadays.

https://preview.redd.it/y97h4l3nu1zf1.jpg?width=752&format=pjpg&auto=webp&s=06e42af3fa7b6dfbd704e02c28c33ab453a54eb1

I don’t think there’s any groundbreaking reason for all this. The elite starters of today simply showed up consistently when it mattered, aided by managers who (mostly) knew best to let them cook—even when they hadn’t shown such longevity during the regular season. For lovers of good pitching outings, this postseason was a treat.

Will we see the crème de la crème of starting pitching shine this brightly in postseasons to come, permanently exiling the era of the bullpen? In light of all of LA’s starters miraculously being healthy at the right time, the unprecedented sensations of Schlittler and Yesavage, sample sizes, and the stagnation of starter workloads, my brain says “probably not.” But my heart says “I hope so.”
