The Rise and Fall of Cecil Travis: The Star Shortstop With More Hits Than 1941 Williams and DiMaggio

#Introduction:

56 hits in a row. .406 batting average. The 1941 season is one of the most iconic statistically in MLB history. But despite the narrative of the American League season being the duel between Joe DiMaggio and Ted Williams and their chases of baseball immortality, another, decidedly less famous player hit the summit of his own career. And despite Ted and Joe’s amazing accomplishments, it was Cecil Travis who took home the AL hit title, clocking in with 25 more hits than DiMaggio and 33 more than Williams.

#Early Years

Coming out of high school already sought after for his bat, Cecil Travis aced the first test posed by minor league baseball. From 1931 - 1933, starting at age 17, Travis hit .356 for the Chattanooga Lookouts. By age 19, all he needed was an opening to make his Major League debut. The Lookouts were a farm team to the Washington Senators, who had not quite yet hit the historical futility recorded by Terry Cashman. Indeed, from 1930 to 1932, the Senators topped 90 wins every season, but pennants evaded them year after year. With a hall of very gooder in Buddy Myer manning second base, and future hall of famers Joe Cronin, Goose Goslin and Heinie Manush manning shortstop and the corner outfield spots, there was only one possible opening for Travis- and that was at third, where team vet and popular defensive star Ossie Bluege stood.

An injury to Bluege gave the 19 year old Travis a chance at a cup of coffee in May 1933. Pulled up, Travis’ first game ever was one for the ages, picking up five hits and scoring three runs in an extra-innings win- the youngest player to ever have 5 hits in a game. But this would be no Lou Gehrig and Wally Pipp story- for the next week, Travis hit .182 and returned to Chattanooga with Bluege’s return. Travis would return to the majors in September as a pinch hitter, but was not part of the Senators World Series roster. He ended his first cup of coffee with an average over .300 though- something he’ll get used to at the MLB level.

Come 1934, the Senators wanted to get their 20 year old young star into the lineup SOMEHOW, and the third base job became his. A sizzling hot start to the season was only interrupted by his career’s first serious threat. After a fastball nailed him in the head, Travis collapsed unconscious. In what was maybe a lucky break, Travis only missed a week recovering from a concussion, finishing out the season. Despite hitting .391 as late as mid-June, Travis dipped down the stretch, ending the year at .319. The Senators themselves imploded spectacularly, finishing 33 games worse than they did the previous season, beginning the road to irrelevance.

As for his batting style, Travis was the stereotypical “pure hitter.” He didn’t walk a lot, he didn’t have power, he didn’t run on the base-paths, but he just hit, smoking his line drives all day. Frankly, for most of his career with his profile… he’s Luis Arraez.

1935 arrived, and Travis found himself in much the same position. A young, talented, 21 year old third basemen on a team that was painfully imploding. There weren’t really any dramatic ups and downs in this season- he came in second on the Senators in WAR, only behind batting title winner Buddy Myer, and began his seven year streak of having more walks than strike-outs in a season- a ratio of 41 to 28. Travis ended the year hitting .318 without as much as a single home run- still slap hitting all day.

1936 saw something different. Not offensively. Travis hit .317 with the same offensive profile as the years before. No, this time, Travis was moved all around the diamond. The Senators of 1936 were far, far different than the stacked line up of 1933. Goslin, Cronin and Manush were gone. Buddy Myer missed large portions of the season with a stomach illness. Young Buddy Lewis took over third, and Travis played… 69 games at shortstop, a new spot and 46 at right field. Throw in a smattering of appearances at left, second and third, and you have a guy with no real home. He was no ace with a glove- the Senators just felt as if they HAD to have his bat.

#Prime Years

1937, and Cecil Travis’ moving days were done. Settling in at shortstop all season, Travis had the finest year of his career so far. Leading the Senators in WAR (of course, a stat super valued in the 1930’s), Travis also hit .344 on the season- 5th in the league, only behind bona fide all timers named Gehringer, DiMaggio, Gehrig and Zeke Bonura. But as things tended to go with Cecil Travis, things didn’t start smoothly. Starting the season with a broken ankle, he didn’t lock into regular playing time until late May. And even then he dazzled- Travis hit in the .380s until August, before he ran into a late season dip once more and couldn’t take home that vaunted batting crown. The Senators however… remained mediocre, despite any heroics. For his efforts, Travis placed 11th in AL MVP voting at the end of the season.

1938 was much the same. Managing shortstop well enough yet again, Travis hit .335 and again placed 5th in the American League. Travis also really filled up the stat sheet by setting a career high in steals (six) and his highest homer total so far (five). He was rewarded with his first ever all-star nod, placing second in the shortstop vote- but his predecessor as Senators shortstop, Joe Cronin, was the AL starter. He placed ninth in the AL in MVP voting at the end of the year, one spot over his own self-declared idol and favorite player- Tigers star Charlie Gehringer. And speaking of the Tigers, they wanted Travis. Badly. And the whole world knew it. Late 1938, rumblings of Travis going to Detroit were abound, to the point you had comics in local Washington newspapers lampooning the deal. Travis’ SABR bio claims that a Travis for Greenberg swap was in the works… but no. A more substantiated rumor had him going to Detroit in exchange for Rudy York, but Greenberg blocked his own friend from going to Washington. Travis is here for the long haul.

Heading into the 1939 season, Travis was still “only” 25, and he was just hitting his stride. What better way to start the season than confined to his bedroom with the flu (or maybe just a “bronchial ailment)? Travis would catch the flu twice that year, and was down in the dumps when he did play, struggling to hit .240 for most of the first half. For once, Travis’ level picked up when the season struck July 1st, getting over the flu to hit a much more in character .327- but for the first time in his professional career, he hit under .300 for a season, falling in at .292. Already thin, the early bouts with the flu really sapped Travis- his SABR bio citing him as losing “considerable” weight. But as the end of the year showed, he had bounced back. And despite his struggles, he was still beloved in Washington. Per the certainly unbiased Cecil Travis Girls’ Club, not only was he the best shortstop in the world, but he was also cute. And those two reasons are more than enough to justify not trading him- not for DiMaggio, not for Bill Dickey, not for Dizzy Dean, and certainly not for whatever player the Tigers toss up this year. The battle between these high school girls defending Travis’ performance and the newspaper bring up trading him would last until the trading deadline that season, but again, he stayed put.

1940 saw Travis move positions once again. Buddy Lewis went to the outfield, and Travis went right back to third base. At the risk of sounding repetitive, his level was back to it’s previous form- even if he only hit .322 this season, Travis set career highs in doubles and triples, with 37 and 11, as well as the top OPS+ of his career so far. Picking up a second all-star game and some down-ballot MVP votes, he seemed what he was.

#1941: The Peak

Heading into 1941, Cecil Travis shook things up. Only aged 27, this is still the time to find your best version. Why not step back in the batter’s box? Why not pick up a heavier bat? Why not change up the grip? The result was his very best version. In a season defined by Ted Williams and Joe DiMaggio, Travis slid right under the radar. The same day that DiMaggio’s streak ended? Travis’ own, much smaller, 24 game streak came to a close. Over the span of Joe’s 56 games, Travis hit a very respectable .356. Travis would lead Ted in the batting race up until the May, when the Splendid Splinter really ramped it up on the way to his most iconic season.

And the Senators were still bad. Well, more like mediocre. The Yankees won the AL… Joe Cronin and Ted’s Red Sox came in second, and the Senators went 70-84 as Travis hit like his life depended on it. Come the end of the season, Travis finished with .359 batting average- ahead of DiMaggio, but still dusted by Williams. His 218 hits though? That not only led the league but led the majors as a whole, the only player to have more than 200 hits that season. Travis set career highs in nigh every stat in this break-through season. Hits. Batting average. 101 RBI. 39 doubles. 19 triples. 7 homers. 150 OPS+. 6.8 WAR. He was especially excellent at night games- hitting .512 in 11 night games, leading the league in that particular stat. At the end of the year, Travis came in a career best 6th in MVP voting. First and second were DiMaggio and Williams. Third was some pitcher named Feller. Fourth was Thornton Lee, the same pitcher who previously beaned Travis in the head, and fifth was Charlie “King Kong” Keller.

Through 1941, his aged 27 season, Travis’ counting stats were strong. So far, 1,370 hits, notably more than legendary “race for 3000 hits” participant Starlin Castro had at that age. A .327 career batting average, primarily as a shortstop and third basemen (more settled into short). Sure, only 25 homers and 20 steals, but it was the average and hits that his legacy would rely on. In terms of advanced stats, his 31.6 WAR certainly wasn’t bad for a pre prime. He was in a similar or better place to contemporary hall of famers at his age- Billy Herman, Luke Appling and even Charlie Gehringer. And if 1941 was a sign, then his career was JUST taking off. Sure, even if he didn’t repeat it exactly, an extended prime in between his 1937/1938/1940 and ‘41 quality would give Cecil Travis as good a shot as any to reach three thousand hits. But he never had the chance.

In December 1941, he lost his appeal and was drafted into the Army, entering WW2.

#The Aftermath

Things went okay for Cecil Travis at first. It wasn’t an action packed start in the army. Sure, a celebrity could join, but he wasn’t exactly sent to the front. From 1942 to 1944, he still traveled around the United States, playing on army baseball teams. But in late 1944, off he went to Europe.

Travis won a bronze star and four battle stars during his service in World War II, but that’s not all he carried. In service during the Battle of the Bulge, Travis would be hospitalized for frostbite. To save the rest of his feet, Travis had to get parts amputated- not an ideal situation if you’re an MLB shortstop. With the end of the war in Europe, Travis would prepare to be re-deployed to the Pacific, but the war ended too quickly, and back to Washington he went.

A three hit game in September would be the only highlight for Travis in his incredibly limited play. He said his timing was gone. And so was his defense- he moved from shortstop to third base. Only recently 32, he very well should have still been playing at a high level.

1946 was Cecil Travis’ last full year as a starter. Power wasn’t there of course, but neither was the hit tool. Hitting .252, Travis also ended with more strike outs than walks for the first time since his rookie year. The Cecil Travis that left for war was no longer here. Not only was his hitting gone, but his fielding was beyond gone. No longer tolerable as a shortstop, the third base experiment did not go well either. He led the league in errors made at third base… while playing 56 total games at the position. Ouch.

1947 saw the plug being pulled, and to the bench he went. His average continued to crater, fighting all season with the Mendoza Line, but he had one final highlight, even if it wasn’t on the diamond. Late 1947, the Senators celebrated “Cecil Travis Night,” to give their old star his great send off. Manager Ossie Bluege (remember that name?) placed Travis back at third, and the miserable Senators showered their veteran with praise, gifts and celebration.

It had a little bit of everything. Dwight D. Eisenhower in attendance and Mrs. Washington paraded about in a float. Over 150 local businesses coming together to raise money and provide gifts, from a new car, to a bull and hunting dogs, a dishwasher and a brand new trailer. An unsolicited gift from the…. umpires on a float. And marching pipe band. What’s not to love?

Travis retired at the end of the 1947 season, spending another decade as a scout before retiring to Georgia. His career stats stand as hardly improved over his from the end of 1941- 1,544 hits, 30.5 WAR, a .314 batting average and 108 OPS+.

#Legacy

For a career that faced adversity and literal war, Travis was always a gentleman about it. Umpire Bill McGowan cited Travis as being the player that always gave him the least trouble and was most admirable in his conduct. Indeed, in the newspapers promoting Cecil Travis Day, they noted that he had never once embarrassed an umpire. Travis never blamed his frozen feet for his failure to pick his bat back up again after World War II- he just said he wasn’t good enough, and lost his timing. Whether that’s the man being humble or the absolute truth, we night never know.

Cecil Travis was never seriously considered for the Hall of Fame. He never appeared on the BBWA ballot. In the mess of the 2007 Veteran’s Committee ballot, Travis placed 22nd with about 15% of the vote- ahead of future hall members like Minnie Minoso, Dick Allen and Joe Gordon, but behind other names like Vada Pinson and Mickey Vernon. Statistically, he just wasn’t up to snuff, no doubt about it. Sure, he had the third highest batting average for a shortstop ever… but his longevity was poor. He didn’t have power. Speed. Defense was hit or miss. And he only had 1,500 hits.

But beyond all of that, you’ll still see me supporting Cecil Travis for the hall of fame. A clearer case of World War II costing someone a hall of fame career probably can’t be seen- if Travis’ level continued, 2000 hits was an absolute lock. Certainly, he’d give 3000 a strong run. His batting average probably won’t dip for years, neither would his defense. Even his advanced stats would probably pull up to be more palatable. But whether it was the four year interruption in general or nearly losing his feet, Travis couldn’t get there. There are a million players who saw injuries cost them their chance at the hall- but how many others saw that come from being drafted into military service?

As they got older and were on the Veteran’s Committee in the 90’s, Ted Williams and Bob Feller would both become firm supporters of Travis’ case. Ted Williams said he was one of the finest left handed hitters he ever saw. Feller said Travis was one of the toughest batters he ever had to face. If I have to be wrong, being wrong alongside Ted Williams and Bob Feller is more than acceptable.
