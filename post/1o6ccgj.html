CHICAGO — Before his team’s third preseason game on Sunday night, Milwaukee Bucks head coach Doc Rivers used his media availability to speak out against the mass arrests by United States Immigration and Customs Enforcement in Chicago.

“It bothers me,” Rivers said. “I’m trying, I’m trying; I mean, it’s just awful what you watch and see, people getting zip tied. I mean, that’s not this country. That’s not what we’re about.”

In the week leading up to Sunday’s game, Rivers made two comments about ICE activity during his sessions with reporters, but saved his most vigorous denouncement of their activity for the Bucks’ visit to his hometown of Chicago.

“I think the mayor’s done an amazing job with everything he says,” Rivers said. “You know, I think this is starting to separate us all, which I hate. This is not; you can be pro-civil rights or what’s right and not be, like, anti-White. And I think that’s what it’s starting to become in some ways. And it shouldn’t be. It really shouldn’t be. I would hope that if this was Ukrainian immigrants being detained, that everyone on both races would fight for it. That’s wrong. It’s just wrong.”

For Rivers, the ICE activity in Chicago has been particularly disappointing because of his family history. Rivers grew up in Maywood, a village to the west of the city, and became a McDonald’s All-American as a basketball player at Proviso East High before attending Marquette University in Milwaukee and eventually putting together a 13-year NBA playing career. His late father, Grady, was a police officer in Maywood for 30 years.

“I think every American is good with, if there’s criminals on the street, we want to arrest the criminals,” Rivers said. “My dad was a cop for Christ’s sakes. My dad would not be proud of this. I know that. My dad would have a major problem; I couldn’t imagine my dad going to work right now and have to protect ICE agents and doing what they’re doing. I couldn’t imagine him wanting to go to work. I think he’d call in sick. And it’s just so — did you walk around the city today? This is an amazing city.”

Rivers went on to tell a story about meeting a married couple that he saw in the elevator of the hotel the Bucks stayed at on Saturday night, who were in the city to support their daughter who ran in the Chicago Marathon on Sunday.

“The couple was so proud, and they were saying, ‘man, this is the best marathon, the city is amazing, I’ve never been to this city, wow,’” Rivers recounted. “And the dad says, ‘but we were so scared, we thought there was civil unrest everywhere.’ And he was like ‘where is it?’ It’s nowhere. It’s just sad. We hate it. We hate it. I’m from Chicago. I’m very prideful about this place, so I hate it.”

While Rivers coaches in Milwaukee, ICE activity has been particularly troubling to him because it has hit so close to home. His brother, Grady Jr., still lives in Elmhurst, a city on the west side of Chicago. An ICE detention facility that has been a site of many protests is located in Broadview, a village that neighbors Maywood to the south.

“It’s awful,” Rivers said. “I know (Broadview) well. My brother lives in Elmhurst. You gotta go from Maywood to Broadview to drive through it. Again, it’s the same thing. And it almost feels like it’s intentional, like they’re trying to create unrest. It wasn’t here, and now it’s here.

“It’s just, there’s gotta be a solution. I mean, protest is legal. You should be able to protest, and I think it’s being made violent in some ways, so I don’t know. Antifa? Does anyone know what that is? I actually looked today. I really did. I actually looked it up and there were 15 different answers for it. Yet we have all these other groups. The Proud Boys, no one’s going after (them). It’s just, this is getting sickening.

“I’m going to say this the last time and then move back to basketball. This should be about the morality of our country and not about the race. This has nothing to do with Black and White. Black and White should be grabbing arms together on this one and fighting against this.”
