Changes in the NBA landscape mean an entirely different team building approach is necessary

The first of these changes is relatively obvious: The CBA, which punishes teams with giant superstar salaries (particularly those with 2-3 of them), and puts a premium on cheap talent, especially those on cost-controlled rookie scale contracts.

The second is less obvious but equally important: The way the league has changed from a physical standpoint. In case you missed it, Thunder GM Sam Presti covered this beautifully in a recent press conference:

https://hoopshype.com/rumor/sam-presti-on-loads-and-injuries-to-think-theres-no-correlation-its-almost-insulting-game-is-totally-different-from-10-years-ago/

In a nutshell, Presti and others argue that faster and more physical style of play that has taken hold in the league in recent years is putting a huge amount of stress on players’ bodies and may be raising the risk of injury among star players. Other factors, such as the “65 game rule” for eligibility for regular season awards or the additional stress resulting from the in-season tournament, are exacerbating this physical stress. (On top of that, think about the extra load on players like Tatum and Brown from playing deep into the postseason every year, plus the Olympics, etc.)

Unfortunately, I don’t see any way that a team can compete for a title in this era without playing that faster, physical, more taxing style of play. Brad Stevens alluded to this in his post-draft press conference, when he praised the style of play in the NBA Finals and gushed about the Thunder and Pacers.

The implications of this for team building are the following, in my opinion:

- Teams should be extremely cautious committing to older players, especially those who have logged heavy minutes over the course of their careers. This is especially the case if we are to believe that the frequency of serious injuries, such as achilles tears, will be higher than in previous generations. Even if we assume older players have the same injury risk as older players, the speed and sheer torque mean that players will start to struggle to keep pace earlier than they used to.

- Depth, especially young depth, is needed in the playoffs more than in the past. Look at what the Pacers accomplished with one star coupled with waves of young talent. Minutes per game need to be limited and a deep bench is key, not just in the regular season but in the playoffs. Picture a top-heavy team like the 2008 Celtics, for instance, with an elite starting five, which may have struggled in this era.

- Teams that take lots of shots in the draft will be better equipped to sustain winning. The old strategy of mortgaging the next several years for the big name, expensive superstar is less relevant now. With the 2nd apron, it will be extremely difficult to field a contending team beyond a year or two without hitting on at least one or two gems in the draft to serve as cheap, high-impact depth.

This is how I see it, at least.
