[Amourban] Deni Avdija: “Yes, sometimes I call myself ‘the silent killer.’ When someone trash talks to me, I try to punish them with baskets. Believe me, it’s the best trash talk there is.”

(Translated from Hebrew to English)

What do you miss most about Israel?

“The warm atmosphere, the family spirit, and the love that exists among our people. Walking around the streets and having conversations with people – you don’t have that in the US, where it’s every man for himself.”

After a long and intense season, don’t you just want to relax and be with your family in the summer?

“Of course I would love to travel abroad and have some more fun, but there’s nothing you can do, you have to sacrifice. I can’t call it a sacrifice either, because the Israeli national team is a huge honor for me, a childhood dream of mine. Getting the opportunity to do great things with the national team is worth every free time you have.”

What would be an achievement?

“The European Championship final. I really want it. I know it sounds unrealistic and that we are a small country and that there are strong teams, but I believe that if we play together and do the things we know, then we can make an amazing move.”

How difficult is it to be away from home since October 7?

“When you’re away, there’s a lot of uncertainty about what’s happening here, and the media doesn’t always reflect that. I try to do as much as I can, to stay updated as much as possible, because ultimately this is my country. So yes, it’s difficult to be away, but on the other hand, I’m very focused on my career, so I try to combine the two.”

What reactions did you get after that?

“At first there was a market in Israel, but after a while people wanted to freshen up and come to the games, to see something a little happier and more optimistic, so I helped as much as I could. Even with the families of the kidnapped, even the disabled IDF soldiers – it did them good to see me play, and that’s the least I can do for people who have experienced trauma.”

Have you ever heard people in the US who don’t really understand what happened in Israel?

“Not everyone understands 100 percent what’s happening in Israel. We’re a very small country, and there are people who live in a bubble in their own countries. A lot of people are interested, and I always explain the whole situation properly, from the right and good side. I try to reflect reality as much as possible.”

Just two years ago, Washington seemed to be banking on Avdija for the long term as a key player, signing him to a new four-year, $55 million contract, as a kind of statement. But in an instant, or rather overnight, the team stunned when they decided to trade him to Portland, with Danny not involved at all.

“People have been by my side for so many years and I’ve formed a close and strong bond with them. The moment they traded me, it felt like a knife in the back,” Avdija says candidly. “I understand that it’s a business and part of this league, but I’m always loyal to the team and the people who worked with me. Starting a new chapter is scary and exciting at the same time. There’s a fear of where I’ll end up and if this will be the right situation for me. You don’t have that much control over it, and that’s what’s scary. But I’m happy because I have the character and the ability to get along anywhere and with anyone. I found an amazing home in Portland, I’m excited to see what happens next.”

What’s the difference between the clubs?

“At the point I was there, I think Portland wanted me more, believed in me more, planned to give me a bigger stage. I really feel like I can do big things with Portland, it’s an amazing club with a lot of fans. The love for this city and the team is immense. I would love to bring them a championship.”

What’s it like playing with a coach like Chauncey Billups, an NBA champion. What does he give you beyond coaching?

“I see the precision in the little details, the fire in his eyes, the drive. He’s a winner at heart. That shows a lot about our style of play. Even against big teams, when we come in as underdogs, we never give up and always play hard. That’s something he instilled in us.”

What do you think about Damian Lillard’s return?

“Excited for him because I know how important the club is to him and how much he loves the city and its fans. Hope he recovers as quickly as possible. I don’t know him personally, but he seems like a great guy who’s fun to play with – hope we do great things together.”

What do you expect to learn from him?

“He had a lot of responsibility, he led the team for years, endured difficult times with the club. He dealt with all the pressure, so I hope he will give me the tools.”

Do you see Portland becoming a top team in the coming years?

“Not only do I see it, but also in the media in the US and objective people who really believe in us. Our roster is fresh and fun to watch. I feel like it is.”

Do you see your future there?

“I see my future with a team that believes in me, that gives me the reins, that loves me and wants me to succeed. Right now, that team is called Portland.”

Can you be a player that a team is built around?

“I’ve proven to myself that I have the qualities to be a player who is seen as very dominant on the roster. I won’t say that I’m God and that they should build around me, but I feel that the team is very considerate of my presence.”

You often find yourself guarding players like Luka Doncic, LeBron James and Kevin Durant. How does it feel?

“It’s tiring. It takes a lot of energy from you. I like challenges. I’m not the most athletic, but I’m competitive and I guard with my heart.”

And attack against them?

“It’s also fun, because sometimes they get lazy and don’t guard as hard. There’s this defense-attack effect, it’s hard to play so many minutes and give 100 percent. You have to know how to bring the energy at the right moments.”

Trash talk?

“Yes, sometimes I call myself ‘the silent killer.’ When someone trash talks to me, I try to punish them with baskets. Believe me, it’s the best trash talk there is.”

Is there any tension with LeBron?

“A lot of people think I have a grudge against him. I really appreciate the person and the player he is, it’s part of the game and part of the fun, I don’t give it too much importance, and I hope he doesn’t either.”

Is there a player you’re still excited to play against?

“No, there isn’t. I’m not excited, but I like playing against the young guys, watching them in their first years in the league.”
