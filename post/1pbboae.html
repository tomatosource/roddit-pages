Which MLB team would be the best if all the teams could only have “hometown” players? (Players born closer to that team’s stadium than any other)

What if teams could only use players born closer to their stadium than to any other stadium?

Turns out this is a question you can answer with some python code!

I ran this analysis last year for the 2024 season, and now that the 2025 season is over, I’ve run it again with updated WAR and a refreshed player pool.

##Methodology

  1. Use Baseball Almanac to identify all 1,463 MLB players active in 2025, and where they were born
  2. Use a geocoder to match their birthplace with a latitude and longitude
  3. For all 1,463 players, calculate the distance from each birthplace to each of the 30 MLB ballparks
  4. Assign each of the 1,463 players to the team that plays nearest to their birthplace
  5. For each team, using that pool of “eligible” players sorted by 2025 WAR, assign:
    a. 5 starting pitchers
    b. 7 relief pitchers
    c. 1 starter each at all 8 field positions
    d. 1 backup catcher
    e. 2 backup infielders
    f. 1 backup outfielder
  6. Fielders are assigned based on their position as listed in the pybaseball 2025 library, but once players are assigned some can be swapped to fill empty gaps or replace a player with lower WAR:
    a. Outfielders are fungible
    b. Corner infielders are fungible (1B and 3B can swap)
    c. Center infielders are fungible (2B and SS can swap)
  7. After all players are assigned and slots are filled, the DH is assigned as the remaining unused player with the highest OPS.

Note that running the analysis using only players active at the MLB level this season means many, many teams do not even have a full roster. Some teams don’t even have a starting 10.

For example, the Giants (who rank quite highly) no longer have an eligible catcher to put on the field (last year they had Curt Casali). The Brewers (who do not rank quite highly) only manage to field 2 infielders and 2 outfielders.

##Results

The results are broken into two tables. In one iteration, I placed no limit on distance from birthplace to stadium. This means basically anyone born in the Caribbean or South America is on the Marlins. Anyone born in East Asia is on the Mariners. Anyone born in Europe or Africa is on the Red Sox. And so on. There are some limitations here that should be apparent with the methodology:

  • Sometimes specific players have random combinations of positions they can play. Like Mark Canha who can play infield or outfield. I didn’t invest the time and energy to capture all these cases while preserving an apples-to-apples WAR comparison so settled on the much simpler “We can swap 1B/3B, 2B/SS, and LF/CF/RF but everyone else gets their pybaseball batting/fielding position preserved”.
  • There’s one guy in particular I had to go in and hard-code in here because my code has an assumption in it I never went in and corrected: that there’s never gonna be a starting pitcher who I also have to analyze offensive WAR for (lol).
  • Regarding the White Sox/Cubs, birthplace is just city. Not neighborhood and certainly not birth hospital. The White Sox get everyone who was born in Chicago, IL even if they’re Northsiders. This is because Rate Field is closer to the pin that OpenStreetMap says is “Chicago” than Wrigley is. This affects the Mets and Yankees to a lesser extent since the dataset I used cites their birth borough and not just “New York City”, so at least between Brooklyn/Queens/Bronx/Manhattan/Staten they get sorted, albeit still imprecisely.
  • This is just birthplace. Sometimes people are born in one place and then move. (Like, for example, Tarik Skubal who grew up in Arizona but tops the WAR list for the Giants because he was born in the Bay Area.) Identifying the high school for every active MLB player and then programmatically assigning each of those high schools a Latitude/Longitude would be a much bigger project than this and I am considering this good enough.
  • This is very literally based on distance from the birthplace to the stadium. This analysis is 100% agnostic of national borders. This means the best player on the Red Sox was born in Montreal and the best player on the Blue Jays was born in Rochester. Sorry, Canada bros.
