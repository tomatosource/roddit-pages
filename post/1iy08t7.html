[OC] On the Sustained Excellence (and Disappointing Stubbornness) of Kevin Durant

PART ONE: Sustained Excellence

In many ways, Kevin Durant’s greatness has been obscured by the shadow of LeBron James. Durant entered the league 4 years after James, and has been playing catch-up in terms of legacy ever since.

It’s difficult to know how many awards and accolades that Durant would have earned without LeBron James around, but there are a few key examples of him finishing second fiddle. In Oklahoma City, he led his team to the Finals (at age 23) – but lost to LeBron James and Miami. He finished second in MVP voting in 2009-10 – but lost to LeBron James. The same thing happened in 2011-12 and again in 2012-13. Kevin Durant finished second overall in MVP voting – but lost to LeBron James. Again. And again.

The other avenue in which Kevin Durant may be overshadowed by LeBron James regards their longevity. Team record aside, Durant is having another great season. He’s still playing at an All-Star level at age 36. The problem is: so is LeBron James (at age 40), so naturally that’s where all the attention drifts.

Still, we should appreciate and marvel at Kevin Durant’s longevity as well. There are signs of decline after all these years and all these injuries, but Durant’s statistical profile is remarkably close to what he’d been during his prime.

Consider this: Durant’s stats ten years ago in Oklahoma City (at age 26) and his stats with Phoenix this season (at age 36).

2014-15 (age 26): 25.4 points per game, 6.6 rebounds, 4.1 assists, 0.9 steals, 0.9 blocks

2024-15 (age 36): 26.9 points per game, 6.0 rebounds, 4.2 assists, 0.9 steals, 1.4 blocks

note: I wanted to pick 10 years since it’s a nice round number, although Durant only played 27 games in 2014-15 so it’s not the largest sample size. his stats in the next full season may be better indicators – a slightly better but similar season (28.2 PPG, 8.2 REB, 5.0 AST).

We all know “per game averages” aren’t the best indication of value, but the advanced efficiency stats are similar as well.

2014-15 (age 26): 57.8% effective FG, 63.3% true shooting

2024-15 (age 36): 59.6% effective FG, 63.9% true shooting

You can nitpick deeper in the numbers and see some decline from his prime, but it’s a remarkably minor drop off given his age.

LeBron James deserves a huge amount of credit for sustaining his excellence – but so does Kevin Durant and Steph Curry and others. They’re defying all conventional wisdom and logic and rewriting history.

PART TWO: DISAPPOINTING STUBBORNNESS

Our world is wild and chaotic, but some things remain the same: like LeBron James and Kevin Durant being All-Star caliber players. All things considered, their numbers are incredibly close to those in their primes.

Kevin Durant has remained as good as he’s ever been (or close to it). He hasn’t changed.

The problem for him is something else has changed…

The league has gotten better around him.

For a ten year period, it’s a striking difference. Thanks to modern analytics and an emphasis on shooting, NBA offenses are playing more efficient basketball than they ever have before. Consider the change in the ten year period of time we’ve examined.

THE NBA AS A WHOLE (2014-15): 49.6% effective FG, 53.4% true shooting

THE NBA AS A WHOLE (2024-25): 54.0% effective FG, 57.4% true shooting

For the entire league to take a 4-5% improvement in ten years is a MASSIVE jump upwards. It’s been a revolution – and offensively speaking, a very successful one.

How did this happen? There are several reasons, but the most striking one is that three-point barrage.

Consider this simple number:

THE NBA AS A WHOLE (2014-15): took 26.8% of their field goal attempts from 3

THE NBA AS A WHOLE (2024-25): have taken 42.0% of their field goal attempts from 3

This jolt upwards is even more striking than the efficiency numbers (and explains a lot about why they’ve happened). Everyone is shooting more threes.

Everyone… except for Kevin Durant. He’s effectively buried his head in the sand, kept his blinders on, and played the same way that he always has.

Kevin Durant (2014-15): 18.4 FGA per game, 6.3 threes per game (34.1% of FGA)

Kevin Durant (2024-15): 18.1 FGA per game, 5.7 threes per game (31.5% of FGA)

Durant hasn’t started increasing of his three-point pace; he’s actually reduced it (by a minor amount). More notable is that, in his prime, Durant took more threes than the average NBA player. Now, he takes less than the average player.

Obviously, it’s still working for him to a large degree. As mentioned, he’s still a great player – still a great shooter – still a great and efficient scorer. He’s still one of the best offensive players in the NBA. That said, I’d argue that he could be even BETTER if he adjusted his game to take advantage of the three-ball in the same way that his peers have done. With Phoenix struggling and on the outside looking in at the playoff picture, it may be time to try and make that adjustment.
