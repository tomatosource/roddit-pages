Inspecting the Contemporary Baseball Era Committee Ballot

Every Baseball Hall of Fame season, the bulk of attention gravitates towards the ballot deliberated by the hundreds of tenured members of the Baseball Writers’ Association of America (BBWAA). This ballot tends to contain ~25-35 names each year, and is the traditional means by which inductions occur. However, a significant proportion of inductions happen in a different way that tends to catch much less spotlight than its effect on Cooperstown suggests it should, and that is via the Era Committee.

The structure of this committee has undergone several changes throughout its history, with its most recent iteration finalized in 2022. Since then, the Era Committee has elected to cycle through three pools of candidates each year: players who primarily contributed to the major leagues before 1980 (Classic), players who did so after 1980 (Contemporary), and non-players also after 1980 (again Contemporary). This year happens to be one in which post-1980 players are considered.

Everything about Era Committee ballots is smaller than BBWAA ones, with the number of voters shrinking from a few hundred to 16, and the number of candidates to a mere 8. Candidates still require 75% of the vote to be inducted, but voters may only choose up to 3 (as opposed to 10 on BBWAA ballots). The voters aren’t revealed until shortly before the results are announced on December 7, which is a practice the institution has come under controversy for due to potential rigging (see HOFers Chipper Jones and Greg Maddux being selected as voters in 2022 when fellow Brave Fred McGriff was inducted).

The eight candidates are:

  • Barry Bonds
  • Roger Clemens
  • Carlos Delgado
  • Jeff Kent
  • Don Mattingly
  • Dale Murphy
  • Gary Sheffield
  • Fernando Valenzuela

Bonds, Clemens, Mattingly, and Murphy were on the last Contemporary ballot (when McGriff was elected). Meanwhile, this is the first time Kent and Sheffield are eligible after long but fruitless journeys with the BBWAA. Lastly, Delgado hasn’t been included in HOF discussions in over a decade, and Valenzuela in over two.

Despite the relatively smaller size of the ballot, each candidate offers a unique story, and predicting the eventual inductees is difficult as a result. This analysis will focus on their statistical merits from a sabermetric perspective (so, ignoring numerical curiosities like hit or strikeout totals), but will also wade into other territory as necessary. For deep dives on each candidate, I highly recommend the prolific write-ups of Jay Jaffe, creator of the JAWS scoring system designed specifically for Hall of Fame use.

Let’s start with simply looking at the players’ career Wins Above Replacement (WAR). I prefer Fangraphs WAR to Baseball Reference WAR in most cases, so we will stick with fWAR for now:

  1. Barry Bonds - 164.5
  2. Roger Clemens - 134.3
  3. Gary Sheffield - 62.3
  4. Jeff Kent - 56.0
  5. Dale Murphy - 44.5
  6. Carlos Delgado - 44.3
  7. Fernando Valenzuela - 43.7
  8. Don Mattingly - 40.7

Barry Bonds is the all-time home run leader with 762.

Already, we are required to address the elephant in the room, considering Bonds’s WAR exceeds that of most combinations of three here. Despite his and Clemens’s undeniable on-field legacies, their candidacies have both been mired by steroids. This controversy—along with other unsavory things about the men themselves—led to them receiving more than half but less than the required 75% in their final years on the BBWAA ballot. Regardless of one’s thoughts on their off-field issues, it would be remarkable if two of the most valuable players in the history of the sport became forever barred from the Hall after this ballot.

So, whether the two “slam dunk” candidates actually receive votes this time (unlike their first Era Committee ballot) remains to be seen. The other two immediate returners—Murphy and Mattingly—also must contend with the possibility of being barred from future ballots (they received 6 and 8 votes last time, respectively). Despite Mattingly’s case being weakest of the bunch by WAR, his candidacy could very well receive more support now that he added a World Series appearance to his coaching resume last month. Murphy’s superior peak may keep him hanging on as well.

Delgado faces a steep climb relative to the field, even if he wins in the character clause department. The same goes for Valenzuela, but that’s if only considering his performance and not the unquantifiable cultural impact he spearheaded for Hispanic and Latino baseball.

An interesting trend is already appearing, with those at the very top of the WAR list carrying significant off-field baggage, but the ones towards the bottom tending to possess off-field boons. This trend is fully buttressed by #3 and #4, who have experienced some controversy in their careers but not to the degree of the top two. Sheffield is at the very least a Hall of Fame-caliber hitter, though his atrocious defense, curtness with the media, and steroid suspicions have bogged down his case for over a decade now. Kent was similarly curt and also struggled with defense (albeit to a much lesser degree), but his bat and lack of steroid controversy lead me to believe he could ultimately be chosen for induction on December 7.

Jeff Kent is the all-time home run leader among second basemen.

Let’s now shift gears to what is perhaps my favorite statistical development in the sport over the last few years: era-adjusted WAR.

The goal of adjusting WAR for era is to account for talent pool discrepancies across time periods. WAR does account for things like park effects and league averages, but it does not account for the impact of integration, for example. A common complaint about WAR is that it overrates older players due to replacement level being lower in those days; eWAR aims to mitigate exactly that.

The work gone into calculating eWAR was extensive as it involved estimating the talent pool of the major leagues—a process suited as much for statisticians as for historians. The main author of the research is Dr. Daniel J. Eck, who collaborated with a handful of colleagues at the University of Illinois to make eWAR a reality. One of these colleagues—historian Adrian Burgos Jr.—happens to be on the Historical Overview Committee that selected the eight candidates we’re discussing right now. Talk about a relevant connection.

Here are the candidates ranked by era-adjusted fWAR (with the difference between their regular fWAR in parentheses):

  1. Barry Bonds - 145.6 (-18.9)
  2. Roger Clemens - 142.3 (+8.0)
  3. Gary Sheffield - 61.2 (-1.1)
  4. Jeff Kent - 55.4 (-0.6)
  5. Dale Murphy - 48.6 (+4.1)
  6. Fernando Valenzuela - 45.9 (+2.2)
  7. Carlos Delgado - 44.4 (+0.1)
  8. Don Mattingly - 41.0 (+0.3)

Fernando Valenzuela won a Rookie of the Year, a Cy Young, a Gold Glove, and even a couple Silver Sluggers.

Changes from regular to era-adjusted fWAR are pronounced among Bonds and Clemens due to their gargantuan totals to begin with. I suspect Bonds’s decreased substantially and Clemens’s increased because of hitting talent being inflated during much of their careers due to steroids. Otherwise, changes are slight, with Murphy and Valenzuela being the only other ones to benefit to any meaningful degree. Since this is a Contemporary Era ballot, era-adjusted totals not differing much from regular totals is to be expected.

Now that we’ve established efWAR’s merits, we’ll stick with it as we further flesh out the candidates’ statistical cases with postseason considerations. Unfortunately, a direct comparison between regular season and postseason stats is difficult on account of WAR not being conceptually suited for October. But, there’s one stat that is a) reliably tracked in the postseason, b) available for both position players and pitchers, c) measured in the currency of wins like WAR, and d) context-neutral like WAR. And that is context-neutral wins, or WPA/LI.

WPA/LI is notoriously hard to explain, but the main idea is that it takes Win Probability Added and adjusts it for leverage. Consider that an at-bat with the bases loaded in the bottom of the 9th with the game tied has very high leverage, so the window for win probability is extreme. Now consider that the batter had no control over the situation being high-leverage or not when he stepped up to the plate. Thus, WPA/LI equalizes leverage across all scenarios in order to be fair to the fact that players cannot control the game state on their own.

Let’s now add each candidate’s postseason WPA/LI to their efWAR to get a more holistic picture of their production:

  1. Barry Bonds - 147.0 (+1.42)
  2. Roger Clemens - 144.8 (+2.48)
  3. Gary Sheffield - 61.9 (+0.67)
  4. Jeff Kent - 56.0 (+0.58)
  5. Dale Murphy - 48.6 (-0.04)
  6. Fernando Valenzuela - 46.9 (+1.02)
  7. Carlos Delgado - 45.0 (+0.62)
  8. Don Mattingly - 41.3 (+0.29)

Carlos Delgado was an MVP runner-up and Roberto Clemente Award winner.

That didn’t really move the needle much, which makes sense considering the small sample sizes inherent with postseason play. Some don’t place any weight towards postseason accomplishments for Hall of Fame cases, but if they did it would only make a marginal difference.

Some also don’t care for candidate evaluations to be completely devoid of context. To introduce context, we can integrate Win Probability Added (WPA) into each of the steps we’ve already made. In the past, I’ve done this for pitchers via Leveraged WAR, in an attempt to more accurately reflect archaic Cy Young voting values. In doing so, I realized LWAR did a better job of aligning the value of relievers with Hall of Fame voting tendencies (i.e., they are valued more by voters than WAR suggests they should be). Despite there being no relievers on this ballot, we may still be able to glean some insight by applying the LWAR methodology here. This involves weighting WPA with WAR at a 3:2 ratio, with it being applied to only the batting component of position player WAR.

The first step is to apply it to standard fWAR (displaying 150% of the pitchers’ totals to place them back on the same scale as the position players). Here are the eight candidates by LfWAR (with the difference between regular fWAR in parentheses):

  1. Barry Bonds - 172.3 (+7.9)
  2. Roger Clemens - 151.2 (+17.5)
  3. Gary Sheffield - 65.8 (+3.7)
  4. Jeff Kent - 53.9 (-2.1)
  5. Dale Murphy - 49.2 (+4.9)
  6. Carlos Delgado - 47.7 (+3.6)
  7. Don Mattingly - 40.7 (0.0)
  8. Fernando Valenzuela - 29.5 (-11.4)

Gary Sheffield debuted when he was 19 and retired when he was 40.

Blending WPA into the equation benefits most players here, though Valenzuela’s meager 4.80 career WPA makes him an extreme exception. Kent also suffers somewhat, widening the gap between him and Sheffield.

Now let’s adjust LWAR for era. Here they are ranked by eLWAR (with the difference between their efWAR in parentheses):

  1. Roger Clemens - 161.0 (+18.7)
  2. Barry Bonds - 152.5 (+6.6)
  3. Gary Sheffield - 64.7 (+3.5)
  4. Dale Murphy - 53.7 (+5.1)
  5. Jeff Kent - 53.3 (-2.1)
  6. Carlos Delgado - 47.8 (+3.4)
  7. Don Mattingly - 41.0 (0.0)
  8. Fernando Valenzuela - 34.0 (-11.9)

Not much different except Murphy is now on equal footing with Kent.

Dale Murphy won back-to-back MVPs.

Lastly, let’s reintroduce the postseason, this time weighting WPA with WPA/LI at a 3:2 ratio:

  1. Roger Clemens - 163.4 (+2.40)
  2. Barry Bonds - 153.8 (+1.28)
  3. Gary Sheffield - 65.2 (+0.47)
  4. Dale Murphy - 53.6 (-0.05)
  5. Jeff Kent - 53.5 (+0.18)
  6. Carlos Delgado - 48.6 (+0.76)
  7. Don Mattingly - 41.2 (+0.24)
  8. Fernando Valenzuela - 35.6 (+1.64)

The purpose of listing all the variations of WAR with or without era, leverage, and postseason play is to show that there are many ways to slice this pie. Each one is perfectly defensible. We could even double the possible variations by including peak à la JAWS, and double that by including rWAR… but that’s probably enough for now.

Don Mattingly has both an MVP and a Manager of the Year award.

To summarize, Bonds and Clemens are obviously in a tier of their own, so voting for them is solely determined by factors other than their play. Sheffield seems decidedly third-best thanks to his bat, which is further emphasized when accounting for leverage. Kent is probably next, though only in a vacuum as Murphy is arguably at his level with era and leverage factored in. Mattingly and Valenzuela probably have the weakest cases on paper, with the back half of the latter’s career being especially detrimental. Delgado’s case is perhaps a bit stronger, but certainly bottom half.

From a purely statistical perspective, the three players to vote for on this ballot seem to be easy choices: Bonds, Clemens, and Sheffield. However, I would bet everything I have that this trio is not elected on December 7 due to aforementioned circumstances. Interestingly, all three received majority support on BBWAA ballots (Bonds and Clemens six times, Sheffield twice), which is usually the best assurance of eventual committee induction. However, not receiving a single committee vote three years ago is not a good sign for Bonds and Clemens. Sheffield’s unique potion of concerns also still lingers; plus, getting elected the first time appearing on a committee ballot is a tall task for anyone.

Roger Clemens won a record 7 Cy Young awards.

Addressing the rest of the candidates necessitates a broader outlook on what a candidacy on this ballot represents. The BBWAA’s Rules for Election state: “Voting shall be based upon the player’s record, playing ability, integrity, sportsmanship, character and contributions to the team(s) on which the player played.” This seems to indicate that one’s playing career (and their personality during it) is the primary if not sole determinant of induction.

But this is not the BBWAA. This is a small committee whose members we don’t yet know, whose nominations we can’t vet, and whose motives remain a mystery as a result. Members of this committee may very well choose to favor aspects of candidates outside the scope of their play, such as Valenzuela’s international icon status, Mattingly’s extensive coaching career, and Delgado’s advocacy. The extent to which these impact votes can’t be known until the results are revealed.

No matter how strong or weak their case is on paper, each of these candidates presents their own perfectly defensible angle to the Hall. On the other hand, none are truly spotless, but such is the nature of failed BBWAA candidates. Whatever the results are, they are sure to surprise, anger, and delight all the same.
