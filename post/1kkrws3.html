[OC] In how many draft classes (of the 2000s) would Cooper Flagg be the # 1 pick?

Tonight, the NBA Draft Lottery will reveal the # 1 pick, and the presumption is that will mean the destination for Duke forward Cooper Flagg. Even though he re-classified and jumped up a year, he still stood out as a freshman as one of the best and most polished players in the country: averaging 19.2 points, 7.5 rebounds, 4.2 assists, 1.4 steals, and 1.4 blocks with good efficiency. With almost no holes in his game, he’s one of the better all-around prospects in years. He’s the overwhelming favorite for the # 1 pick.

The question here is: in how many classes would he have gone # 1? It’s almost an impossible question to answer. How can you compare different team needs? Different eras? It’s a fool’s errand.

As luck has it, I am indeed a fool, so I’ll take a stab at it here. Let’s go year by year starting with 2000 and compare the # 1 pick to Flagg.

2000: New Jersey selects PF Kenyon Martin (Cincinnati)

Kenyon Martin had been seen as the best college player all season long. A high flyer with great defensive intensity, Martin dominated the block with 1.4 steals and 3.5 blocks per game, winning the Wooden Award. Still, Martin didn’t fit the bill of most top NBA prospects. He only really broke out as a senior (with 18.9 PPG). Prior to that, he had averages of 2.8, 9.9, and 10.2 points per game. He didn’t have much offensive skill beyond dunking – and he didn’t have the overwhelming size (at 6’9”) to suggest he’d continue to score at a high level in the NBA either. To make matters worse, he’d be recovering from a broken leg. The younger Cooper Flagg would be an obvious pick here.

2001: Washington selects C Kwame Brown (high school)

In the era of high schoolers going straight to the pros, Kwame Brown was a late riser (compared to his peers like Tyson Chandler and Eddy Curry). Apparently, he dominated in workout settings given his size and athleticism. However, he was still an unproven commodity compared to Cooper Flagg who has shown to be productive at the NCAA level. Barring a strong team “need” for a true big, Flagg seems like the likely pick.

2002: Houston selects C Yao Ming (China)

This is a tricky one. At the time of the 2002 draft, there was more of a consensus that Yao Ming would be the # 1 pick than there was that he was the best prospect in his class. He was an enormous talent and a marketing bonanza, but many pundits were skeptical about how well his athleticism would translate in the States. Basketball wise, prospect Flagg would be seen as a safer bet in a vacuum. But would ownership get involved here and demand Yao Ming? TBD. This is the first time I’ll throw my hands up and cop out with a toss up.

2003: Cleveland selects SF LeBron James (high school)

Like Kwame Brown and Yao Ming, high schooler LeBron James was technically unproven. But obviously, his combination of otherworldly athleticism and basketball IQ had made him one of the top prospects of all time regardless. He gets a comfortable win over Cooper Flagg here.

2004: Orlando selects C Dwight Howard (high school)

Like Kwame Brown, Dwight Howard was a relatively late riser who ended up securing the # 1 pick based on his athleticism in workouts. He even jumped past NCAA champion Emeka Okafor, who had been seen as the presumptive top pick for most of the season (and averaged 4+ blocks per game for three straight years at UConn). I may lean slightly to Cooper Flagg here based on proven production, but Howard’s video game athleticism would make teams think twice and maybe make this another toss up. And remember, a year after LeBron James, the stigma about high school kids had started to dissipate.

2005: Milwaukee selects C Andrew Bogut (Utah)

Andrew Bogut was a good college player – and got selected at the top of a strong draft crop that included Chris Paul (who went # 4). His stats at Utah (20.4 PPG, 12.2 RPG, 1.9 blocks) could be stacked up against Cooper Flagg, but there’s a key distinction that Bogut was a sophomore and Cooper Flagg is an underaged freshman. Most general managers would give his youth the benefit of the doubt and the nod based on upside.

2006: Toronto selects PF Andrea Bargnani (Italy)

Andrea Bargnani wasn’t seen as the consensus top prospect in his class – and was viewed as an unpopular pick even at the time. However, some of that grumbling may have been due to the failure of recent top Euros, notably Darko Milicic (the # 2 pick in 2003). On paper, Bargnani had more of a resume than Milicic. He averaged 12 points on good efficiency (39.9% from 3) in Italy’s top league and had been named EuroLeague’s “Rising Star” prospect of the year. Still, in most classes, he’d have been drafted around 2 or 3 and not # 1 like Cooper Flagg.

2007: Portland selects C Greg Oden (Ohio State)

Greg Oden had perhaps the most similar trajectory to Cooper Flagg: they both went wire to wire from high school to the NBA Draft as the # 1 prospects in their class – leading their college teams to the Final Four along the way. Among the two, I’m giving the nod to Greg Oden here (presuming he wasn’t medically red flagged). Cooper Flagg is a good defender, but a traditional shot blocking center like Oden (who averaged 3.3 blocks per game) can anchor your defense in a more fundamental way. At the time, most thought he’d be a likely DPOY and a potential Hall of Famer.

2008: Chicago selects PG Derrick Rose (Memphis)

Like Greg Oden and Cooper Flagg, freshman phenom Derrick Rose led his college team all the way to the Final Four (although none of the three actually won the title). He had a perceived upside beyond the stats (14.9 PPG, 4.5 RPG, 4.7 APG) with athleticism and good PG instincts. That said, “size matters” in the NBA Draft – especially in the older eras. Teams generally go with the bigger body like Cooper Flagg on the expectation that they can affect the game in more ways. Again, team need may have swayed the vote though.

2009: L.A. Clippers select PF Blake Griffin (Oklahoma)

Here’s another tough call. As a freshman, Blake Griffin had been projected as a potential top 5 pick. But he decided to return to college and put away any doubt, absolutely dominating to the tune of 22.7 points and 14.4 rebounds per game. However, I’d say Cooper Flagg has a slight edge as a prospect given his youth and all-around skill set. In two years of college, Griffin went a grand total of 3⁄10 from three and only hit 58.9% of his free throws.

2010: Washington selects PG John Wall (Kentucky)

The debate between Derrick Rose and John Wall (both products of Coach John Calipari) would have been fascinating. As freshmen, Wall’s college stats were a little better (16.6 PPG, 6.5 APG, 1.8 SPG), but his super team at Kentucky busted in the Elite Eight against a West Virginia team that included some plucky guard named Joe Mazzulla. Rose vs. Wall is a coin toss, so we’ll have to give Wall vs. Flagg the same result here and lean to Cooper Flagg. In both cases, it may again come down to a matter of “need.” I suspect PG-needy teams may have pulled the trigger on either Derrick Rose or John Wall over Cooper Flagg if they had a hole there.

2011: Cleveland selects PG Kyrie Irving (Duke)

As far as freshmen point guards go, Kyrie Irving had a skill advantage over both Derrick Rose and John Wall. However, he had a more limited athleticism that (at the time) translated to “lower upside” from NBA scouts. Fellow Dukie Cooper Flagg will get the win here.

2012: New Orleans selects PF/C Anthony Davis (Kentucky)

This is among the harder debates on the board. As freshmen, Cooper Flagg’s offensive stats are better than AD’s. However, AD’s defensive numbers (including 4.7 blocks per game) and superior length and athleticism would suggest a higher upside. A pre-draft workout between them would have been fascinating, but I suspect it may have gone Anthony Davis’s way. He had the rare athleticism of a Dwight Howard coupled with results and an NCAA title.

2013: Cleveland selects PF Anthony Bennett (UNLV)

The Cavs raised eyebrows and shocked Bill Simmons with their pick. But we can’t confuse the surprise of Anthony Bennett being # 1 – or disappointment of his future “bust” career – with our exercise of considering his value at the time of the draft. He had been slotted more in the # 5 range, but was still viewed as a good prospect overall. He was seen as an athletic stretch four (who hit 37.5% from three), albeit likely someone who would be your 2nd or 3rd option. His eventually weight/asthma issues also weren’t a big red flag at the time, and he had been projected as a good defensive player. Alas, he’s not in the same league as a more polished Cooper Flagg.

2014: Cleveland selects SF Andrew Wiggins (Kansas)

Back in high school, Andrew Wiggins was viewed as one of the best prospects since LeBron James, with wiry athleticism and All-Star upside. I would say that his freshman year was slightly underwhelming though, shooting just 44.8% from the field. Statistically, Cooper Flagg trumps him across the board, but the desire for a high-flying wing is always tempting. Let’s call this another toss up.

2015: Minnesota selects C Karl-Anthony Towns (Kentucky)

There’s a big difference between Karl-Anthony Towns the prospect and Karl-Anthony Towns the NBA star. Both of them are great – but in different ways. We all know KAT as one of the best shooting bigs of all time, but he only shot 2⁄8 from three over his entire freshman season. Instead, defense was supposed to be his calling card after leading a 38-1 Kentucky team to the top defensive rating in the NCAA. He wasn’t an athletic marvel like Dwight Howard or Anthony Davis, but he was supposed to be an anchor down low regardless. It’s debatably a toss up, but I’d lean to Karl-Anthony Towns for this reason… in 1-on-none pre-draft workouts, it’d be easier to see KAT’s shooting upside and harder to notice his lateral defensive limitations.

2016: Philadelphia selects PG/SF Ben Simmons (LSU)

Coming into college, Ben Simmons would have stacked up with Andrew Wiggins as two of the most hyped prospects of the post LeBron era. He put up some spectacular individual numbers in college, including 19.2 points per game, 11.8 rebounds, 4.8 assists, and 2.0 steals. In hindsight the most amazing stat of all is that he dared to go to the line 9.0 times per game! That said, he wasn’t seen as a flawless prospect even back then. There was some concern about his shooting, of course, but more concern about his attitude and will to “win.” He eschewed bigger programs for a friendly deal at LSU, but he didn’t even lift the Tigers into the tournament. Gilbert Arenas’ gun to my head I’d lean Simmons but it’s close enough to default to another toss up.

2017: Philadelphia selects PG Markelle Fultz (Washington)

For the second straight year, Philadelphia got the # 1 pick – and for the second straight year, they drafted a freshman whose team missed the NCAA Tournament. That said, Markelle Fultz appeared to be a sure-fire stud to everyone but Danny Ainge: with all-around averages of 23.2 PPG, 5.7 RPG, 5.9 APG, 1.6 SPG, 1.2 BPG. The only real red flag was his FT% at 64.9% and his shaky interviews. There are no off-the-court concerns with Cooper Flagg so let’s give him a narrow victory here. You can argue this may be falling victim to revisionist history and disagree though.

2018: Phoenix selects C Deandre Ayton (Arizona)

The “size matters” and center bias led Phoenix to Deandre Ayton over Luka Doncic. At the time, it didn’t feel as obviously foolish. Ayton had the natural talent and the resume – 20.1 PPG, 11.6 RPG as a freshman – that suggested All-Star potential. In hindsight, everyone claims they had Luka Doncic # 1, but the only two teams whose big boards we know (Phoenix and Sacramento since the Kings accidentally tweeted it) both had Ayton at the top of the class. Let’s go with a probably-unpopular toss up here.

2019: New Orleans selects PF Zion Williamson (Duke)

Stats wise, Cooper Flagg may be most similar to fellow Duke freshman Zion Williamson. However, Zion Williamson had a notable advantage in terms of excitement, athleticism, and “star power.” I don’t think there were many GMs in the league that were going to pass on that.

2020: Minnesota selects SG Anthony Edwards (Georgia)

Anthony Edwards turned out to be an All-Star, but at the time it was no guarantee. In college, Anthony Edwards was inefficient across the board. He only shot 40.2% from the field, only 29.7% from three. Worse yet, his Georgia Bulldogs couldn’t even break .500, going 16-16 and missing the NBA tournament. Most teams viewed him as a boom-or-bust prospect who would hopefully become the next Dwyane Wade, but potentially could turn into the next Dion Waiters or Andrew Wiggins as an empty calorie scorer. As a safer bet, Cooper Flagg surely would have gone ahead of him as prospects.

2021: Detroit selects PG Cade Cunningham (Oklahoma State)

The jumbo point guard Cade Cunningham held on to his # 1 status, clipping Evan Mobley and Jalen Green. The combination of his size for the position, his shooting numbers in college (40.0% from three, 84.6% from FT), and his all-around basketball character graded him highly. In terms of athleticism, he had been viewed as below-average compared to most # 1 picks though. I’d lean slightly to Cooper Flagg but this could be another toss up.

2022: Orlando selects PF Paolo Banchero (Duke)

Once again, Cooper Flagg will go head to head with another Duke PF. If you saw them in a gym, you’d lean to the bigger and bulkier Paolo Banchero. But based purely on stats, it’s hard to say Banchero was a better prospect than Cooper Flagg. Banchero had a good season (17.2 PPG, 7.8, 0.9 BPG) but it wasn’t quite on the level of Flagg’s. There was also some concern that Banchero was “maxed out” already, which is a criticism that we’re already hearing about Duke’s next superstar PF Cam Boozer.

2023: San Antonio selects C Victor Wembanyama (France)

If you base the prospects on perceived value at the time, you can make an argument for Victor Wembanyama over anyone else, even LeBron James (as blasphemous as that sounds). But there’s less debate about the fact that those two tower over all others, including Cooper Flagg.

2024: Atlanta selects SF Zaccharie Risacher (France)

As a prospect, Zaccharie Risacher may compare similarly to another # 1 pick like Andrea Bargnani. He also played in a top European league and held his own as a teenager. His skill set (as a 6’10” shooter) also boded well for a long future in the NBA. However, in terms of raw upside and athleticism, he’s low on the list for most # 1 picks and would have fallen behind Cooper Flagg if eligible.

OVERALL FINDINGS

These are all open for debate and criticism, but my list shows these results…

Cooper Flagg goes # 1 in : 14 classes (Kenyon Martin, Kwame Brown, Andrew Bogut, Andrea Bargnani, Derrick Rose, Blake Griffin, John Wall, Kyrie Irving, Anthony Bennett, Markelle Fultz, Anthony Edwards, Cade Cunningham, Paolo Banchero, Zaccharie Risacher)

TOSS UP in : 5 classes (Yao Ming, Dwight Howard, Andrew Wiggins, Ben Simmons, Deandre Ayton)

Cooper Flagg does NOT go # 1 in: 6 classes (LeBron James, Greg Oden, Anthony Davis, Karl-Anthony Towns, Zion Williamson, Victor Wembanyama)

We only ranked # 1 prospects, but depending on how you feel about the close calls and toss ups and premium # 2 picks like Kevin Durant, it could be fair to suggest that Cooper Flagg would in the top 10 prospects of the 2000s, likely around the 7-10 range.
