How good would Jerry West be in the modern NBA? An analysis.

Any discussion of Jerry West should only be held after seeing him play. Unfortunately, the video quality from his era is terrible, making it hard to appreciate his game.

That said, we do have HD footage of Jerry West dominating in Game 4 of the 1970 NBA Finals, which offers a glimpse into just how big of an issue the man was for defenses of the era.

with that out of the way… let’s get some background info and establish some facts. First:

Jerry West was a Bucket

Jerry West’s scoring achievements in the 1960s and early 1970s are rightfully revered by fans today, even when the achievements of his contemporaries are not. His career average of 27 PPG — and 29.1 PPG in the post-season, putting him third all-time — keeps Mr. West in the discussion today for the greatest scoring point guards of all time.

There are many reasons modern fans still regard Mr. West so highly. But perhaps the greatest reason of all is the fact he was a walking bucket when all buckets were worth two points. And despite his scoring feats all coming without a 3 point line, Mr West also put up gaudy numbers in an era with tighter defensive rules and stringent dribbling restrictions.

He was also efficient and versatile.

Mr. West shot 47.4% from the field for his career (excellent for a guard in his era) and was an ~81% free-throw shooter. He could get you buckets pulling up off the dribble, coming off screens for catch-and-shoot jumpers, finishing fast breaks, and driving to the basket to draw fouls (evidenced by an NBA-record 840 free throws made in 1965–66). He was essentially a proto “combo guard” in an era before the term existed – capable of running the offense with his playmaking and simultaneously serving as his team’s primary scoring option.

Inevitably, when discussing Jerry West, we arrive at a singular question:

How much could he have scored if he played in the three-point era?

Several factors support these projections:

  • Extra points from range. Mr. West was already taking and making shots well beyond what became the three-point arc. Assigning an extra point to a few of those attempts each night easily adds two or three points to his scoring average. Analysts estimate he would have been a 42–43% three-point shooter – elite by modern standards
  • Era adjustments. The 1960s featured a blistering pace (120+ possessions per team per game at times) but relatively inefficient offenses (league average was 93.6 points per 100 possessions in 1962). Today’s NBA runs fewer possessions, but with far higher efficiency (110+ points per 100). Adjusting for this shift shows that stars like Mr. West would maintain or even increase their per-possession output (NBA.com pace & efficiency archive).
  • Usage. In years when teammates were hurt, Mr. West carried nearly half his team’s scoring load. In the modern NBA, where lead guards often shoulder 30–40% usage, Mr. West would be expected to take just as many shots – but with better spacing and no hand-checking, he would likely be more efficient.

Playoffs. If his regular season scoring rises 10–15%, his playoff averages likely would too. That projects his career playoff average to around 32 PPG, good for second all-time behind only Jordan (Basketball Reference). His record 40.6 PPG postseason in 1965 might have topped 45 with threes in play.

Adapting to Today’s Game

Offense

Mr. West’s offensive profile would fit seamlessly into the modern NBA.

  • Three-point weapon. His pull-up range was already beyond the line; today he’d shoot threes off the dribble and off screens, similar to Steph or Dame, while his projected 42–43% from deep would place him among the best shooters ever.
  • Mid-range and versatility. Like KD or Booker, Mr. West would very likely be deadly from the mid-range. He was also excellent off the ball, capable of filling a Ray Allen/Klay Thompson role running off screens (The Ringer).
  • Playmaking. Later in his career, Mr. West averaged nearly 10 assists per game (Basketball Reference). In today’s spread pick-and-roll offenses he’d function as an intiator for the offense, while remaining a potent scoring threat.
  • Transition. Coming from a fast-paced era, Mr. West thrived in the open court. In today’s game he could easily pull up for transition threes.
  • Clutchness. I mean, he was already known as “Mr. Clutch”. In today’s era, his late-game shooting would only be more devastating with the three in play.

Comps

  • Stephen Curry: offensive gravity and shooting.
  • Damian Lillard: deep range and clutch shot-making.
  • Kobe/Jordan: mentality, mid-range dominance, playoff scoring.
  • Ray Allen/Klay Thompson: off-ball movement and catch-and-shoot ability.
  • Chris Paul/Luka Dončić: ability to run an offense as a high-usage creator.

Conclusion

Mr. West in the modern NBA would be a mother-f*cking PROBLEM. Morseso than he was in his own era. With the three-point shot, his career average climbs near 30 points per game, his playoff average into the low 30s, and his peak seasons well into the mid-30s. That puts him in the rare company of Jordan, Wilt, and KD.
