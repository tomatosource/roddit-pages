KALK BLOCK: why Charlotte rookie Ryan Kalkbrenner may be worth keeping an eye on

New Charlotte center Ryan Kalkbrenner (the # 34 overall pick) won’t be the most hyped or discussed rookie in his class, but he may be one of the more interesting case studies to keep an eye on.

If you follow college basketball, you’d know Kalkbrenner as a great college player at Creighton. But for many NBA fans and pundits, that’s where the interest may have ended. For skeptics, he’s too old (after 5 years in college). He’s too skinny. His skilled post game won’t translate against the better, bigger athletes of the NBA. Maybe he’s just a great “college player” and that’s it.

Certainly, Kalkbrenner checks the box of a great college player. As a sophomore, he averaged 2.6 blocks per game and won Big East “Defensive Player of the Year.” The next year, he won it again. The next year, he won it again. The next year, yes, he won it again. He’s a 4-time Defensive Player of the Year in his conference.

Of course, many centers are going to be impactful on defense. Many centers are going to be shot blockers.

Is that really worth a post? Probably not.

What does this all mean?

Modern fans are smart enough to know now that “blocks” does not automatically mean “good defense.” Similarly, we’re smart enough to know that “avoiding fouls” (or your block/foul ratio) doesn’t automatically mean “good defense” either. There’s a lot more that goes into being a good defensive center than that.

However, Kalkbrenner’s insane ratio does suggest a sense of good basketball intelligence and timing on his blocks. He’s managing to be effective there while avoiding fouls – which helps him stay on the court. Some of the top shot blockers in the league fit this pattern. In fact, there were only 2 qualifiers in the entire NBA that recorded more blocks than fouls (above a 1.00 ratio). They were Victor Wembanyama (1.64) and Walker Kessler (1.07). Kalkbrenner’s college ratio of 1.87 was even higher than those two entering the draft (with Wemby at 1.38 and Kessler at 1.78). It’s not the highest ever (Anthony Davis was over 2.0, for example) but it shows you how elite that number is.

Will Kalkbrenner’s college success translate to the NBA in the same way? That’s the million-dollar question.

I’d argue against the idea that his age (as a 5th-year senior) is aiding him a lot here. He recorded around the same positive block/foul ratio in all 4 seasons that he’s started in college. A more legit knock may be against his level of competition. The Big East isn’t elite (and pales in comparison to European pro leagues, naturally). Creighton also relied on Kalkbrenner to log heavy minutes, so perhaps he managed his fouls more than he would in another circumstance.

But overall, the biggest question mark is still whether there is a fundamental difference between college basketball and the NBA. Zach Edey and Donovan Clingan drew more fouls (proportionally) in the NBA than they did in college, suggesting that perhaps visible and explosive athleticism is important after all.

Ryan Kalkbrenner will be an interesting test of that, even if no one will be paying attention.
