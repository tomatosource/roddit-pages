Cuban’s main point is that Torre is overstating the idea that the $50M investment was meant for cap circumvention. He argues that the money wasn’t directed to Kawhi Leonard at all, and that Torre is focusing too much on timing coincidences. Cuban explains that contracts paid quarterly naturally align with investment rounds, so payments will almost always fall close to a closing date… that doesn’t prove wrongdoing by itself.

He then dives into how carbon credits work, noting that Aspiration wasn’t selling a physical product but rather promises of future credits once trees were planted or projects verified. Putting up cash allowed Aspiration to start that process, and Cuban emphasizes that the economics of this system are complicated. He points out that large companies like Meta and Bank of America were involved in similar credit purchases, and it would’ve been nearly impossible to scam all of them. From his view, the money likely went toward legitimate credit purchases and sales growth, not a scheme to funnel cash to Kawhi.

Cuban also questions Torre’s reliance on internal finance sources who admitted things didn’t make sense to them. He says these employees escalated their concerns to senior management, but those were the same people who had already signed off on the deals, making it circular reporting. He stresses that the only person who truly understood what was going on was Sanberg, since he controlled the money and decisions, not the senior execs Torre cited.

He further points out that Aspiration’s auditor, KPMG, resigned, and the company even hired a law firm to investigate, which found nothing. To Cuban, this shows that even experts brought in at the time didn’t uncover fraud, so Torre shouldn’t lean so heavily on hindsight or incomplete info. He also highlights Sanberg’s deposition, where Sanberg took the Fifth, which Cuban interprets as unusual since Sanberg supposedly didn’t care about the CBA or salary cap.

Finally, Cuban argues that everyone but Sanberg believed Aspiration still had value in 2022. Sanberg alone knew the stock was worthless, which made him the key figure. Cuban suggests Torre’s reporting downplays this, painting the scandal as a systemic cap circumvention effort rather than a case of one person (Sanberg) controlling money, hiding losses, and misleading everyone else, including creditors.
