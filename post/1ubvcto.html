It’s not easy to average 20 points per game in the NCAA. The college game is shorter (40 minutes) and clunkier. Among the college stars (and future # 1 picks) who did NOT average 20 include Derrick Rose, Kyrie Irving, Anthony Davis, Anthony Edwards, Paolo Banchero, Cooper Flagg, etc.

In fact, among all the top 3 picks in the 2000s, only 15 players averaged over 20 PPG in college. (Note: not all top 3 picks played in college, to be fair. Only 61 did.)

This year, we may add 3 more to the list. No one’s claiming that this year’s crop has a generational prospect on the level of a LeBron James or Victor Wembanyama, but the richness of the top 3⁄4 is rare. They’re all freshmen who hit the ground running in college. SF A.J. Dybantsa (BYU) averaged 25.5 points. SG Darryn Peterson (Kansas) averaged 20.2 despite missing chunks of games. PF Cam Boozer (Duke) looked like a ten-year pro, averaging 22.5 points. PF Caleb Wilson (UNC) barely missed the club, stalling at 19.8 points per game.

If you combine the four, they averaged 88.0 points in college. That’s the highest mark among any quartet in the 2000s. The only draft class that threatened them was 2019, where the top three (Zion Williamson, Ja Morant, R.J. Barrett) all averaged 20+ as well.

Now, as mentioned, not every top pick played NCAA basketball. We had several jump straight from high school prior to the rule change. We’ve had others come over from Europe. Only 12 draft classes had 4 NCAA players at the top.

If you go back and check the averages from those other respective leagues (high school, international, G League, etc), this 2026 class would still rank second among the 2000s. And yes that’s including some gaudy high school stats. The only class that can beat the current one is 2002, buoyed by the 32.4 points that Yao Ming averaged in China.

Does this mean the top 4 in this draft class is the BEST quartet in the entire 2000s? No, not necessarily. There’s more to the game than scoring averages alone. Also, one superstar talent (like Wembanyama) can probably lift a draft class on his own.

But if we want to rank draft classes based on the quality of their 2-3-4 range, these classes would come to mind:

2003 : This is clearly the cream of the crop no matter how you slice it. You had LeBron James at # 1, followed by a hyped # 2 in Darko Milicic and a proven stud in # 3 pick Carmelo Anthony. I believe that Anthony (coming off a 22⁄10 NCAA title year) would have been the # 1 pick in an average draft class. To add to the surplus of talent, we followed it up with Chris Bosh and Dwyane Wade.

2007: At the time, a healthy Greg Oden was considered a potential Hall of Famer. Kevin Durant was the runner-up here, but his college resume suggested a future All-Star. The 3-4 weren’t as strong, but Al Horford and Mike Conley Jr. both projected to be good and steady starters.

2014: Both Andrew Wiggins and Jabari Parker were both labeled as future stars for most of their amateur careers. They were joined by Joel Embiid, who was rocketing up the charts prior to a leg injury. # 4 pick Aaron Gordon could be an even comp for Caleb Wilson although I’d argue in prospect Wilson’s favor.

2015: Like the year before, we had two big-name prospects battling for the top spot in Karl-Anthony Towns and Jahlil Okafor. They were joined by the productive D’Angelo Russell (who went # 2), and the upside international unicorn in Kristaps Porzingis.

2018: Revisionist history claims that Luka Doncic was an obvious # 1 prospect here, but we forget that Deandre Ayton and Marvin Bagley were hyped up as well. They both averaged 20⁄10 as college freshmen. Jaren Jackson Jr. was more untested at # 4 and may downgrade this class a bit.

2019: Zion Williamson gave the class a signature star, and Ja Morant and R.J. Barrett were both 20+ PPG scorers in college as well. That’s a very strong top 3. # 4 pick De’Andre Hunter was a clear notch below that, although some saw him as a potential Kawhi Leonard type.

Where does the 2026 rank among all these quartets? I think you can make the argument that it’s top 3.
