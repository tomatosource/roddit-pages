Extra Innings: A Dinger x Mrs. Met fanfiction. Chapter 2.

Link to Chapter 1 if you need to catch up

Marriage, Mrs. Met had learned, was not a dramatic thing.

It was not shouting or slammed doors or tearful ultimatums. It was quieter than that. More surgical. It was the slow normalization of absence, of not being asked how your day had been, of not being noticed when you came home with your hands still shaking from holding a smile too long.

She sat at the kitchen table in Queens, the overhead light buzzing faintly. Across from her, Mr. Met scrolled through his phone, thumb moving in an unbroken rhythm.

“Do you ever think about leaving?” she asked.

The question slipped out before she could stop it.

He looked up, startled, not by the words, but by the fact that she’d spoken at all.

“Leaving what?” he asked.

She searched his face for something.

Fear.

Curiosity.

Anything that suggested the question had landed where she’d aimed it.

“Never mind,” she said quietly.

He nodded.

“Long season. Everyone’s tired.”

That was always the answer.

She watched him go back to his phone and felt the familiar, crushing sensation of having failed a test she hadn’t known she was taking.

Later that night, when he fell asleep beside her without touching her, she lay awake staring at the ceiling and wondered when companionship had started to feel lonelier than solitude.

Her phone buzzed. She didn’t hesitate.

In Denver, Dinger was pacing his living room, tail swishing with restless energy. He’d told himself he wouldn’t wait by the phone. Had failed almost immediately.

Did I wake you? she texted.

I was already awake, he replied. I usually am.

She pictured him, barefoot, oversized frame folded into a space too small for him.

I asked him something tonight, she wrote.

What kind of something?

The kind you ask when you’re hoping the other person will notice you’re drowning.

Dinger stopped pacing.

Did he?

She stared at the blinking cursor.

No.

The word felt heavier than it should have.

On his end, Dinger sat down hard on the couch.

I’m sorry, he typed.

She swallowed.

Don’t be. I think part of me wanted him not to.

That was the truth she hadn’t admitted out loud before. That the marriage felt like a room she’d outgrown, and she was standing hunched inside it, bruising herself on the walls just to prove she was still there.

That makes you human, Dinger replied.

_____________________________________________

The dependency crept in quietly. At first, it was just timing, learning when the other would be free, adjusting sleep schedules to overlap by a few stolen minutes. Then it was emotional calibration: waiting to tell stories until she could tell him, measuring the weight of her day by whether or not she’d get to share it. When a game went badly, Dinger was the first person she wanted to hear from. When the crowd felt thin and unenthusiastic in Denver, she was the one who reminded him he still mattered.

They never said need. They didn’t have to.

One afternoon, Mrs. Met found herself standing in front of her closet, staring at the familiar lineup of Mets-blue and orange. She touched the fabric absently, then stopped. She realized she’d been wondering, without meaning to, what Dinger would think of her in something else. Something that wasn’t a uniform. Something that didn’t belong to anyone but her.

The thought made her nauseous with guilt. She closed the closet door too hard.

In the living room, Mr. Met looked up. “You okay?”

“Yes,” she said automatically. Lie number six that day.

That night, she texted Dinger something she’d been holding back for weeks.

I feel like I’m becoming someone I don’t recognize.

He took longer to respond. When the message came, it was careful.

Do you feel like that when you’re with him?

Her fingers trembled.

No.

Another pause.

Do you feel like that when you’re talking to me?

She pressed the phone to her chest, eyes burning.

No, she typed. I feel like myself.

Dinger closed his eyes. That was the moment he understood the danger. Not the attraction. Not the longing.

The relief.

Days later, the Mets and Rockies both had off-days in the same city for once, Chicago.

Different hotels. Different schedules. Same weather. Same air.

They didn’t plan to see each other. They didn’t even talk about it.

But when Mrs. Met stepped into a quiet coffee shop near the river and saw a flash of purple fur in the corner of her vision, her heart stuttered violently, like it was trying to escape her chest.

Dinger froze too.

For a moment, neither of them moved.

Then he stood.

They didn’t touch.

They didn’t hug.

They barely even spoke.

They just stood there, too close, the space between them humming with everything they refused to acknowledge.

“You shouldn’t be here,” she whispered.

“I know.”

“I can’t-” She stopped, breath shaking.

“I can’t do this.”

He nodded, even as his hands curled into fists at his sides.

“I don’t want to be your escape,” he said quietly. “I want to be your choice.”

Her eyes filled. “That’s what scares me.”

They separated without saying goodbye. She cried the entire walk back to her hotel.

That night, lying beside her husband, Mrs. Met stared into the dark and realized something devastating:

She wasn’t staying because of love. She was staying because leaving would require wanting herself enough to choose differently.

And somewhere between Denver and Queens, she’d started to believe that the only place she could breathe was in messages from a dinosaur who wasn’t hers to lean on.

________________________________________________

The first thing Mrs. Met noticed when the messages stopped wasn’t the quiet. It was the way time stretched.

Mornings became longer, heavier. She woke before her alarm and reached instinctively for her phone, thumb hovering where his name should have been.

Each time, she caught herself, heart already racing, body already braced for the small mercy of connection, and each time, she felt the same dull drop in her stomach.

Nothing.

She told herself it was for the best. That’s what people said when something hurt in a way that sounded moral.

At breakfast, Mr. Met talked about logistics. Travel dates. Appearances. A charity event she’d already forgotten agreeing to. She nodded at the right moments. She did not tell him that she felt like she was watching her own life from underwater.

In Denver, Dinger deleted the draft he’d written for the fifth time.

You don’t have to reply.

Delete.

I’m thinking about you.

Delete.

I’m trying to do the right thing and it feels like losing a limb.

Delete.

He set his phone face down on the counter and pressed both hands flat against the granite until the cold bit into his palms. This was what he’d said he would do. Step back. Stop being the thing she leaned on when the marriage hollowed her out. Stop letting himself be the quiet place she went to feel real. He’d meant it.

What he hadn’t expected was how physical the absence would feel. The restlessness. The constant urge to narrate his day to someone who no longer answered. The way the stadium felt impossibly large without the knowledge that somewhere, far away, she understood exactly why that mattered.

At Coors Field, the crowd cheered, and he waved and danced and pretended. Later, alone in the locker room, he sat with his head in his hands and waited for the shaking to stop.•

Mrs. Met lasted four days.

On the fifth, she broke down in a supply closet beneath the stadium, crouched between boxes of promotional foam fingers and unused banners from seasons that promised more than they delivered.

Her phone buzzed. For half a second, her heart surged with relief so sharp it bordered on pain.

Then she saw the name.

Not his.

A text from Mr. Met.

Running late. Don’t wait up.

She slid down the wall and let her head fall back, a sob tearing out of her before she could stop it. The sound startled her, it felt too loud, too raw for someone who’d spent years perfecting quiet endurance.

“This is ridiculous”, she told herself. “This is inappropriate. This is emotional infidelity dressed up as friendship.”

Knowing that didn’t make it stop hurting.

That night, she typed a message she never sent.

I don’t know how to exist without you in the margins of my day.

She erased it. Then typed another.

Please tell me this gets easier.

Erase.

Finally, she locked her phone and placed it face down on the nightstand, as if that might keep the longing from leaking out.

Withdrawal did strange things to her.

She laughed less at work. Missed cues. Forgot choreography she’d known for years. A staffer asked if she was sick. She almost said yes. She started noticing things she’d been insulating herself from with Dinger’s presence: the way Mr. Met never reached for her hand anymore, the way their conversations never drifted past surface-level logistics, the way she felt relief, not love, when he left the room.

One evening, as they sat eating dinner in silence, Mr. Met glanced at her and frowned.

“You’ve been distant,” he said.

The words landed harder than she expected.

“I’m fine,” she replied, reflexively.

He studied her for a moment longer, then nodded.

“Okay.”

The ease with which he accepted the lie felt like confirmation of everything she was afraid to admit.

In Denver, Dinger started talking to himself.

Not out loud, never that, but in the same mental cadence he used when he talked to her.

Observations. Jokes she would have appreciated. Small, private thoughts that no longer had a place to land. He caught himself reaching for his phone during a rain delay and laughed bitterly when he realized what he was doing.

This is what you wanted, he reminded himself. This is restraint. This is respect.

It felt indistinguishable from grief.

One night, after a loss that left the stadium deflated and quiet, he sat alone in the dugout long after everyone else had gone. The field lights cast long shadows across the dirt, and for a moment, he imagined her sitting there beside him, legs swinging, sharing the silence like it was something precious instead of empty.

The image hurt enough that he stood abruptly and left.

They broke the silence by accident.

A late-night text sent to the wrong contact. Muscle memory, cruel and automatic.

Do you ever feel like you’re still waiting for permission to want more?

Mrs. Met stared at the screen in horror when she realized what she’d done. She didn’t unsend it. She couldn’t.

Three minutes passed. Then her phone buzzed.

Every day.

She covered her mouth with her hand, breath hitching, tears spilling freely now.

I’m sorry, she typed quickly. That wasn’t fair. I shouldn’t have-

Don’t, Dinger replied. I needed to hear that more than I’m willing to admit.

They didn’t talk long. Just a few careful messages, each one wrapped in apology and restraint. But when the conversation ended, neither of them felt better. They felt relieved.

And that terrified them both.

Later, Mrs. Met lay awake beside her husband, staring into the dark. She understood something then, with a clarity that made her chest ache: She hadn’t been withdrawing from Dinger. She’d been withdrawing from the version of herself who could survive without being truly seen.

And now that she knew what that felt like, the silence wasn’t healing her.

It was erasing her.
