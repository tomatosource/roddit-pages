What the 2025 NPB season actually looked like before Murakami, Imai, and Okamoto came over

By the end of the 2025 season, Nippon Professional Baseball had made one thing unmistakably clear: the best teams were the best teams.

&nbsp;

And yet, somehow, nothing felt resolved.

&nbsp;

The Japan Series felt anticlimactic in a very specific way—unless you’re from Fukuoka. Hanshin didn’t lose in chaos or collapse. They lost slowly and cleanly. One-run games. Extra innings. No failure, just separation between a very good team and a machine that has defined Japanese baseball for much of the 21st century.

&nbsp;

That was the year in miniature. Hanshin, under first-year manager Kyuji Fujikawa, didn’t drift toward the Central League title so much as collide with it. From late June onward, they won eleven straight games and turned the rest of the season into a formality. They clinched the pennant earlier than any team in league history. Teruaki Sato finally crossed the line from promise to superstardom, hitting 40 home runs and winning Central League MVP. Daichi Ishii followed with one of the most extreme relief seasons you’ll ever see: fifty straight scoreless appearances and a 0.17 ERA.

&nbsp;

In the Pacific League, SoftBank took a different path. They stumbled early, looked briefly broken in April, then calmly posted the league’s best record every month afterward. Livan Moinelo was the best pitcher in Japan—1.46 ERA, elite command, Pacific League MVP—and by the time the Hawks reached Hanshin in the Japan Series, the ending felt less surprising than inevitable.

&nbsp;

The offseason didn’t resolve much either. It just moved the pieces. Munetaka Murakami left Yakult for Chicago. Tatsuya Imai went from Saitama to Houston. Kazuma Okamoto, Tokyo to Toronto. Kohei Arihara quietly shifted the Pacific League’s balance by leaving SoftBank and returning to Nippon-Ham.

&nbsp;
