Did the New York Yankees LIE about beating a group of Englishmen in a game of cricket? An investigative report

I’m a writer in Bermuda and thought I’d share this little nugget I found while researching for a book I am writing.

The brass tacks are thus:

In 1913, while the New York Yankees and Jersey City Skeeters were in Bermuda for Spring Training, insults were exchanged between local cricketers and American baseball players.

According to American records, the locals, while watching the Yankees train, began insulting the game of baseball.

Yankees players then challenged them to a game of cricket to prove baseball’s superiority, declaring they’d beat the English at their own game—which they did.

Though far from a well-known story, this version of the story is what has gone down in history.

As someone who played both cricket and baseball—and a proud Bermudian—I think it’s a load of boohickey. And I can prove it.

But first, some historical context.

In the early 1900s—as Bermuda is becoming a burgeoning hot-spot for American tourists—our biggest tourist attraction, a horse racing track, was shut down. This caused much consternation among the public, who wrote in to our local newspaper, The Royal Gazette, bemoaning the lack of any entertainment for Americans visiting the island. At some point, someone suggested we begin playing baseball by taking advantage of preexisting cricket infrastructure. And so we did exactly that.

In 1907, the first game of competitive baseball was played in Bermuda. In the newspaper’s report of that game, it was explicitly stated that the goal was to entertain visiting Americans and, perhaps one day, lure a Major League ballclub to our shores for Spring Training.

In the spring of 1913, that exact plan came to fruition as the Highlanders, soon to be known as the Yankees, arrived in Bermuda. This, of course, brought with it a whole lot of press coverage. Among the reporters who came to Bermuda to report on the Yankees training was a journalist from the New York Tribune: “The Princess of the Pen”, Harriet Underhill. Ms. Underhill would go on to become one of America’s most important female journalists, but here in Bermuda, I fear she got away with a minor bit of fibbing.

WHAT THE SOURCES SAY

Here is an excerpt from the article in question, published in the Tribune on March 11th, 1913. I have bolded the aforementioned fib for your convenience:

At this minute of writing Hal Chase is on the Hibiscus covered balcony reading Gwendolyn’s Lovers; Russell Ford and Ed. Sweeney, the handsome twins, are explaining to some newly initiated ‘fans’ the difference between a spit ball and the other kind, and George McConnell is strolling on the lawn hunting for a four leaf clover.

Some Englishmen who were watching the men at practice yesterday said: ‘This baseball looks a very stupid game. I suppose you never saw a game of cricket, did you?’ And the men like true Americans, answered: ‘No, but we can beat you at it,’ which they promptly did.

This struck me as rather improbable. How could baseball players beat a team of cricketers when they’d have no clue how to bowl a cricket ball? 

My suspicion would later be confirmed when I came across an article published here in Bermuda in 1956, in which the local man responsible for connecting Bermuda with Frank Chance’s Yankees was interviewed.

In that interview, he too recounted a game of cricket between the locals and visiting ballplayers. Only his recollection was that the game was between locals and the Jersey City Skeeters, not the Yankees.

The Sports Editor of The Royal Gazette learned from Mr. Jack Watlington, a member of the Hamilton Second XI cricket team thirty years ago, that the Jersey City baseball team which wintered here at that time made this mistake much to their discomfort.

After decrying the British national game, the International League team agreed to play a game of cricket with the local eleven.

Jersey City scored about 60 runs, according to Mr. Watlington’s recollection, and then took the field. They found the bowling difficult, not being able to take account of the fact that a spin ball breaks faster on the ground than in the air. The Hamilton team finally had to lend them a couple of bowlers.

The home team lost a couple of wickets until Harry Tucker and Jack Watlington partnered. When they were at the wicket the score was crossed, and from then on the runs mounted steadily.

After about an hour of this, the ball players found they had to go and attend baseball practice.

So What Really Happened?

Let’s look at the sources.

Source A:

Harriet Underhill – New York Tribune**, March 11, 1913 (U.S.)**

“Some Englishmen who were watching the men at practice yesterday said: ‘This baseball looks a very stupid game. I suppose you never saw a game of cricket, did you?’ And the men like true Americans, answered: ‘No, but we can beat you at it,’ which they promptly did.”

  • Reports that locals insulted the game of baseball
  • Frames the event as Yankees vs. Englishmen/locals, and says the Yankees won.
  • No score given. No details about bowling, overs, wickets, etc.
  • Highly narrative, extremely “picturesque,” clearly emphasizing American bravado.

Source B:

Jack Watlington – Royal Gazette**, Bermuda (interview, 1956)**

“After decrying the British national game, the International League team agreed to play a game of cricket with the local eleven.”

  • Says that the baseball players insulted the game of cricket
  • Identifies Jersey City Skeeters, not the Yankees.
  • Gives a score (~60 runs)
  • Says the Skeeters were struggling to understand cricket technique (spin, bounce).
  • Locals were winning comfortably when the game stopped.

Historical Context

Early 1910s newspapers, particularly those owned by Hearst and Pulitzer, routinely exaggerated the facts, or made them up entirely, to feed whatever narrative suited the paper best. This era (1910–1915) is known for:

  • “Color writing” over factual reporting,
  • Yellow journalism hangover, and
  • Newspapers selling narrative over accuracy.

Harriet Underhill, brilliant writer though she was, was part of a field that rewarded storytelling. Indeed, her prose reflects this nature, reading more like a travel column, not a box score.

Most Likely Version of Events

There was one cricket match. It was against the Jersey City Skeeters. The locals won.

Harriet Underhill reshaped the story — turning minor-leaguers losing at cricket into Yankees winning — because it made a better, more patriotic narrative for New York readers.

Now, does any of this matter? Of course not. I’m probably one of very few people in the world who know of this supposed cricket game in the first place.

Did the Yankees lie about it? No, that was just a clickbait tile to reel you in. How’s that for Yellow Journalism, Harriet?

That is all.
