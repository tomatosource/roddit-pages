[OC] Are Slumps Inevitable? A Deep Dive Using Every Batting Champ Since 1949

Background

Back in June, these stories^(1)^(,)^(2)^(,)^(3) were published during Aaron Judge’s “slump”.

“That’s baseball,” Judge said, per ESPN’s Jorge Castillo. “We know what we signed up for. You’re going to play 162. You’re going to hit a little rut like this, but you can’t give up. You can’t mope about it. You just got to show up the next day and you got to be ready to play.”

I wanted to do a deep dive into slumps to see if Aaron Judge was correct when he said, “that’s baseball.”

On the surface, it makes sense. With 600–700 at-bats per season, of course a hitter will go through a slump. People here will remember Chris Davis’ historical 54 at-bat hitless streak in 2018–19, but that got me wondering:

What should we expect from baseball’s best hitter each year?

Method

Theoretical approach

To get a general feel for how long of a hitless streak I should expect, I wrote a Python simulation and looked into the math behind longest streaks. I’m not a math major, so I used Perplexity:

The expected length of a player’s longest hitless streak can be approximated as:

E[max streak] ≈ ln(1−BA)/-ln(n)

where:

  • n = number of at-bats
  • BA = batting average

Worked example

Ichiro Suzuki, 2004

  • BA: .372
  • ABs: 704

Using the formula, we’d expect a longest hitless streak of ~14 at-bats.
His actual longest slump was 12 at-bats.

The Python simulations pointed in the same direction, so I moved on to real data.

Empirical approach

I used Retrosheet play-by-play data to pull every plate appearance from 1949–2025. Seasons before 1949 were excluded due to incomplete data.

For each season:

  • I identified the batting average leader
  • Tracked all at-bats
  • Found the first occurrence of their longest hitless streak
  • Logged the results in an Excel table

Analysis

Finding #1: Tony Gwynn

This stood out the most.

Tony Gwynn won five batting titles, and his average longest hitless streak was 9.8 at-bats, well below the dataset average of ~13.

He owns two of the three shortest slumps ever by a batting average leader:

  • 8 ABs in 1987
  • 9 ABs in 1994

Other multi-title hitters like Miguel Cabrera and Wade Boggs typically fell in the 12–14 AB range.

Finding #2: The 1958 Richie Ashburn outlier

Richie Ashburn’s 1958 season produced the longest hitless slump in the dataset:

  • 28 at-bats
  • Nearly double what his batting average would predict
  • ~4.6% of his season spent in one hitless stretch

Conclusion

Nothing here completely overturns how we think about slumps, but that was kind of the point.

Even the best hitter in baseball:

  • Will usually go 12–15 at-bats without a hit
  • Occasionally reach 20+
  • And very rarely avoid slumps altogether

I mostly came away appreciating how good Retrosheet’s data is, and how absurd Tony Gwynn’s consistency really was—especially after seeing some discussion recently about him being overrated.

TL;DR

That’s baseball.
