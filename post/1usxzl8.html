Why players taking discounts (Brunson, Wemby rumors) means ownership won the Game Theory war against the NBPA

We’ve all seen the praise Brunson got for taking his massive extension discount (which was actually less about competing and more about his offers, but is often framed a roster building choice that others should make) and the recent rumors that Wembanyama might consider something similar down the line to help San Antonio build.

Fans love it. It gets framed as “sacrificing for the team” and chasing a legacy. But if you look at the game theory of the NBA ecosystem, players taking discounts to circumvent the cap means ownership has effectively won the war against labor.

  • The Death of Parity Mechanism #1: The Draft

The NBA has two main tools to enforce parity: the draft lottery and the salary cap. The draft has already been heavily subverted. For years, bad teams abused the system by tanking. The league’s recent aggressive fixes (flattening the lottery odds) were meant to curb this. It may eliminate tanking (we shall see), but it absolutely will diminish the efficacy of the draft and lottery as a parity mechanism. Now, it’s genuinely harder for the truly terrible teams to get the top pick, while average or borderline play-in teams are incentivized to throw late-season games just to slide into a flat-odds lottery slot.

With the draft no longer reliably saving bad teams, all the pressure shifts to the second mechanism: the salary cap.

  • The Cap is Ownership’s Shield Against Blame in the Name of Parity

In a league without a hard salary cap, owners are forced to pay top dollar. If a team sucks, fans blame the owner’s lack of will to spend. To counter this, ownership pushed for stricter parity tools like the new 2nd apron.

The 2nd apron was meant to kill superteams by forcing talent to distribute naturally. Instead, it’s created a landscape where GMs are still handing out bad max contracts out of fear, relatively starving role players, and putting massive pressure on top-tier stars to bail them out financially.

  • The Illusion of the “Good Teammate”

When a star takes a discount, they win the PR game. But they lose the broader labor war by establishing a toxic narrative: If you actually care about winning, you should sacrifice your income.

Historically, the NBPA (the players’ union) has fiercely resisted this mindset. The entire point of the union is to maximize player revenue and protect the collective market. When a superstar takes \(30–\)40 million less, they depress the market ceiling, giving ownership immense leverage to pressure the next guy into a team-friendly deal. And this plays out not just at the top, where generational wealth resides, but also down the line, where if the best player is willing to do this for the time, then surely everyone else should be as well, and the players should just be thankful for a roster spot on a competitive team in negotiations.

Furthermore, if we genuinely want parity, stars taking discounts completely subverts it. It unevenly weighs top-tier talent relative to their cap hit. A contender with a top player on a discounted contract gets an artificial competitive edge that the actual rules of the cap were explicitly designed to prevent. It’s great if it is your team alone, it sucks if it is a rival team.

  • Why This is Game Theory in Practice

This dynamic is a classic Prisoner’s Dilemma. For an individual player, defecting from the union’s collective goal (maximizing salary) to take a discount yields a massive individual payoff: better teammates, fan adulation, and higher championship odds.

But when individual stars act in their own self-interest this way, it creates a dominant strategy that destroys the collective bargaining power of the entire group. Ownership didn’t just design the system; they successfully manipulated the incentives so that the players’ optimal individual move actively works to suppress player salaries as a whole.

  • If Everyone Does It, Only the Billionaires Win

Think about the end-game of this trend:

Scenario A: Only a few stars do this. The teams whose players play by the rules get penalized for not taking discounts, and parity is ruined anyway.

Scenario B: Every star in a winning window starts doing this. If every contender has players taking discounts, the competitive field flattens out anyway. This means the only actual change is that billionaire owners save tens of millions of dollars.

As fans, it makes sense to cheer when our star takes less, because it lets our team subvert the cap. But structurally, public and moral pressure forcing players to subsidize their own rosters means the players completely lost the game theory match. They are paying for the league’s “parity” out of their own pockets while the owners pocket the savings.

Edit: It’s been fairly pointed out that players earn a set percent of league revenue. This is still a game being played, but now it’s also the billionaires playing against each other, as the individual decision making on a roster by roster and player by player basis are still varying due to this process. In short, teams aren’t playing evenly, so some teams benefit more and other less.
